## A teacher from Bilopillia

Anton Makarenko was born in 1888 in the small town of Bilopillia (now in the Sumy region). In 1905 he finished the city school and teacher-training courses in Kremenchuk and began teaching in schools across Ukraine. In 1917 he graduated from the teachers’ institute in Poltava.

## A colony for the kids no school wanted

After years of war and revolution, many children were left without parents or homes. In 1920 Makarenko set up the Maxim Gorky Labor Colony for homeless children and young offenders in the village of Kovalivka near Poltava. Later the colony moved into the former Kuriazh monastery near Kharkiv. From 1927 Makarenko also ran the Dzerzhinsky Commune on the outskirts of Kharkiv, and from 1932 to 1935 he was its deputy head. Many of the children there had been left homeless by the Holodomor of 1932–1933.

## Brigades, uniforms and self-government

Makarenko’s central idea was that children are raised by the collective. His pupils were split into brigades that competed with each other, and the children took part in running the colony. There were military-style uniforms, daily rituals and group discussion of misbehavior; only the director could hand out punishment. To critics who said the collective crushes individuality, Makarenko replied that in a socialist society the interests of the group and the person become the same.

## Books the world read

Makarenko told the story of the colony in A Pedagogical Poem (1933–1935), followed by A Book for Parents (1937) and Flags on the Towers (1938). As a writer he was influenced by Maxim Gorky, who wrote to him and met him. Makarenko wrote only in Russian, but his books were translated into 58 languages.

## Kyiv, the NKVD and Moscow

In the summer of 1935 Makarenko moved to Kyiv to work in the labor colonies department of the Ukrainian NKVD, and from October 1936 he was acting head of NKVD colony No. 5 in Brovary. In 1937 he moved to Moscow, where he wrote fiction and works on education. He died of a heart attack in 1939.

## Debates and assessments

Centers in Germany, Italy, Japan and other countries study Makarenko’s legacy, and an International Makarenko Association was founded in 1991. His system also has critics, who write that order in his collectives rested on peer pressure, hierarchy, paramilitary rituals and the director’s right to punish, and that the children were constantly reminded their collective was part of the Soviet one. A separate question is his service in the NKVD during the years of terror. In 2024 an expert commission of the Ukrainian Institute of National Memory, citing an archival file, concluded that Makarenko had worked for the NKVD and that place names and monuments in his honor are propaganda of Russian imperial policy. Researching his legacy and reading his books remain legal.

## Why it matters today

Educators in many countries still discuss Makarenko’s ideas about children’s self-government and learning through work. His story also shows how a gifted teacher from the Sumy region ended up serving the Soviet system. There is a Makarenko museum in Kremenchuk.
