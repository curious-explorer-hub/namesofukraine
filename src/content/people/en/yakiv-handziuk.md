## A giant with eight stripes

Imagine an officer more than two metres tall, with a thick beard and eight gold stripes on his sleeve, one for every wound. That is how his fellow officers remembered Colonel Yakiv Handziuk. In the First World War he often led his regiment into an attack himself, ahead of his men.

## A soldier's son from Podillia

Yakiv Handziuk was born in 1873 in the village of Bahrynivtsi in Podillia (now the Vinnytsia region), into the peasant family of a retired artilleryman. Other sources say he was born in Lozove, in today's Khmelnytskyi region, into a priest's family. He graduated from the Odesa Infantry Cadet School and served in the russian army. In the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–1905 he led his soldiers out of an encirclement holding the regimental banner, and his record in the First World War made him a major general in 1917.

## The first Ukrainian division

In 1917 Handziuk commanded the 104th Infantry Division in the corps of [Pavlo Skoropadskyi](/en/people/pavlo-skoropadskyi/). He was one of the first on the Southwestern Front to Ukrainianize his division, which became the 1st Ukrainian Division of the 1st Ukrainian Corps. In his memoirs Skoropadskyi called him a true hero. When Skoropadskyi resigned at the end of 1917, Handziuk took over the corps.

His troops held the railway junctions of Shepetivka, Rivne and Zdolbuniv, and in December 1917 they disarmed a Bolshevized Guards corps that was heading for Kyiv.

## The refusal

In February 1918 Mikhail Muravyov's Bolshevik troops took Kyiv, but Handziuk did not know it. He set out from his headquarters in Bila Tserkva for Kyiv with his chief of staff, General Yakiv Safonov, and Colonel Oleksandr Haievskyi. Near the city a Bolshevik roadblock seized them and took them to Muravyov, who offered the generals a place on the Red side. They refused. On 9 February 1918 (other sources say 10 February) Yakiv Handziuk and Yakiv Safonov were shot in Kyiv. Haievskyi, wounded, escaped and told what had happened.

In the summer of 1918 the generals were reburied with honours at the Vydubychi Monastery in Kyiv. The Soviet authorities destroyed the grave; it was restored after Ukraine regained independence.

## Why it matters today

Yakiv Handziuk was a general in someone else's army, but in 1917 he chose Ukraine and turned his division into a Ukrainian force. As the Ukrainian People's Republic was being born, his regiments were among the few fighting units that shielded its capital. Even facing death he would not betray that choice, and his loyalty still inspires Ukrainian soldiers. Since 2020 the 59th Brigade of the Armed Forces of Ukraine has carried his name.
