## Who he was

Volodymyr Ivasiuk was born in 1949 in Kitsman in Bukovyna, into a family of language and literature teachers; his father, Mykhailo Ivasiuk, was a writer. As a child he learned the violin and piano, and at 15 he set up a girls’ vocal group at school, Bukovynka, and wrote songs for it. In 1966 the family moved to Chernivtsi. After the incident with the Lenin bust, Ivasiuk was expelled from the medical institute; he worked as a fitter at the Lehmash factory, and in 1967 he was readmitted to the institute while also studying at the music college.

## His story

In 1970 Ivasiuk wrote “Chervona Ruta” and “Vodohrai.” They premiered on 13 September 1970 on Theatre Square in Chernivtsi, in a live TV broadcast, with the composer singing a duet with Olena Kuznetsova. His best-known songs were performed by the band Smerichka and its soloists Vasyl Zinkevych and [Nazarii Yaremchuk](/en/people/nazarii-yaremchuk/): with “Chervona Ruta” they won the all-Union TV festival Pisnia-71, and “Vodohrai” won Pisnia-72. In 1971 the musical film Chervona Ruta came out, starring Sofia Rotaru and Vasyl Zinkevych, and in 1974 Rotaru sang “Vodohrai” at the international song festival in Sopot, Poland.

In 1972 Ivasiuk moved to Lviv. He graduated from the medical institute (1973), and from 1974 he studied composition at the Lviv Conservatory, first under Anatolii Kos-Anatolskyi. He wrote songs to his own lyrics and to poems by Rostyslav Bratun, Bohdan Stelmakh and Dmytro Pavlychko: “Dva Persteni,” “Ya – Tvoie Krylo” (I Am Your Wing), “Klenovyi Vohon,” “Pisnia Bude Pomizh Nas,” “Balada pro Malvy.” He composed the music for a stage version of The Standard-Bearers, a novel by [Oles Honchar](/en/people/oles-honchar/), at the Zankovetska Theatre in Lviv, as well as instrumental pieces: 107 songs and 53 instrumental works in all.

On 24 April 1979 Ivasiuk left home after a phone call and never came back. On 18 May his body was found hanged in the Briukhovychi forest near Lviv; the exact date of his death is unknown. On 22 May he was buried in Lychakiv Cemetery in Lviv. Although the authorities tried to prevent a mass farewell, more than 10,000 people came to the funeral.

## Debates and assessments

The official Soviet verdict was suicide: as early as July 1979 the prosecutor’s office closed the case “for lack of a crime.” Few people believed this from the start. His family insisted he had never spoken of suicide; his former girlfriend Tetiana Zhukova claimed the opposite in a 2009 documentary. The Internet Encyclopedia of Ukraine, published in the West in 1988, says the circumstantial evidence points to the KGB; the Ukrainian encyclopedias VUE and ESU say he died in unexplained circumstances.

In February 2009 Ukraine’s Prosecutor General’s Office reopened the case. The 2009–2012 investigation ended in November 2012 with the case closed again “for lack of a crime”; in June 2014 the Prosecutor General’s Office overturned that decision as unfounded and resumed the investigation. In 2019 the Kyiv Scientific Research Institute of Forensic Examinations, at the request of the Lviv regional prosecutor’s office, carried out an investigative experiment and concluded that Ivasiuk could not have hanged himself unaided. No one has been officially named as responsible; the VUE notes that the investigation is ongoing.

## Why it matters today

Ivasiuk showed that a Ukrainian song could be modern and deeply national at the same time: his melodies blend Hutsul and Pokuttia folk music with beat-music rhythms. The all-Ukrainian Chervona Ruta festival (since 1989) is dedicated to his memory; among others, the young [Kuzma Skriabin](/en/people/kuzma-skriabin/) performed there and the band Okean Elzy won it. Posthumously Ivasiuk received the Shevchenko Prize (1994) and the title Hero of Ukraine (2009); monuments to him stand in Kitsman and Lviv, and one of the Lviv monuments (2011) was put up with the support of patron [Svyatoslav Vakarchuk](/en/people/sviatoslav-vakarchuk/).
