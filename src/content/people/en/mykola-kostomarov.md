## The serf who became a professor

Mykola Kostomarov was born in 1817 in the village of Yurasivka in the Voronezh Governorate, now in Russia’s Voronezh Oblast. This land once belonged to the Ostrohozk Cossack regiment of Sloboda Ukraine, founded by Ukrainian settlers. The boy was the illegitimate son of a Russian nobleman and a Ukrainian serf woman, so he was born a serf himself. Ukrainian culture surrounded him from childhood. He finished secondary school in Voronezh and, in 1836, Kharkiv University.

## Iieremiia Halka

In Kharkiv Kostomarov became fascinated by the past of Sloboda Ukraine, and then by the age of [Bohdan Khmelnytskyi](/en/people/bohdan-khmelnytskyi/). At the same time he began writing in Ukrainian: his collections Ukrainian Ballads (1839) and The Branch (1840) came out under the pen name Iieremiia Halka. The university banned the defence of his first dissertation, on the church union. He defended a second one, on the historical meaning of folk poetry, in 1844.

## The secret brotherhood

In 1846 Kostomarov became an adjunct professor at Kyiv University. There he grew close to Panteleimon Kulish, Vasyl Bilozerskyi, Mykola Hulak and [Taras Shevchenko](/en/people/taras-shevchenko/), and together they founded the secret Brotherhood of Saints Cyril and Methodius. Kostomarov became its ideologue and wrote its main programme, The Books of the Genesis of the Ukrainian People. He saw the future of the Slavs in a free union modelled on the United States. In March 1847 the members were arrested; Kostomarov was taken on the eve of his wedding to Alina Kragelska. He spent a year in the Peter and Paul Fortress and was then exiled to Saratov and barred from teaching and publishing. He and Alina finally married in 1875.

## History is not just about tsars

In 1859 Kostomarov became professor of Russian history at Saint Petersburg University, but in 1862 he left in protest at the authorities’ actions during student unrest. From then on he only researched and wrote. His books on Bohdan Khmelnytskyi, the Ruin and [Ivan Mazepa](/en/people/ivan-mazepa/) drew on documents he tracked down in 65 archives and libraries. He wrote lives of [Volodymyr the Great](/en/people/volodymyr-velykyi/), Petro Mohyla, [Ivan Sirko](/en/people/ivan-sirko/), [Pylyp Orlyk](/en/people/pylyp-orlyk/) and [Hryhorii Skovoroda](/en/people/hryhorii-skovoroda/), and got readers interested in Ukrainian history. Kostomarov believed a historian should study the life of the whole people, not just its rulers, and he founded the populist trend in Ukrainian historiography. In his essay Two Rus’ Peoples (1861) he argued that Ukrainians and Russians are different peoples with different histories.

## Shevchenko’s Kobzar

Kostomarov and Shevchenko had been friends since the days of the brotherhood. In 1867, after the poet’s death, a Kobzar came out that Kostomarov had compiled and edited and for which he wrote the preface. It was the first posthumous edition of Shevchenko’s works.

## Why it matters today

Kostomarov died in Saint Petersburg in 1885. His ideas about democracy and federation shaped not only historical scholarship but also the Ukrainian national movement. [Mykhailo Drahomanov](/en/people/mykhailo-drahomanov/) and [Mykhailo Hrushevskyi](/en/people/mykhailo-hrushevskyi/) wrote about him, and Hrushevskyi called some of his essays “canonical”. Kostomarov’s books still help readers see in Ukraine’s history not only hetmans but ordinary people too.
