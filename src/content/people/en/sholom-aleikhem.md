## The boy from Pereiaslav

Sholem Rabinovich was born in 1859 in Pereiaslav, in the Kyiv region, into a Jewish shopkeeper’s family. He spent his childhood in the nearby small town of Voronkiv. Later he turned his memories of it into Kasrilevke, an invented town that became the image of the Jewish shtetl. He studied at a kheder (a religious primary school) and then at the Pereiaslav district school, which he finished with distinction. Around that time, still a teenager, he wrote his first story, in Russian: A Jewish Robinson Crusoe. He gave Russian lessons, and from 1877 he spent a few years as a tutor in the family of Elimelekh Loyev, a wealthy leaseholder, where he fell in love with Loyev’s daughter Olga. When the romance came out, he had to leave. In 1881–1883 he was the state-appointed rabbi in Lubny.

## “Peace be with you!”

In 1883 his first works in Yiddish came out in a St Petersburg weekly, among them the novella Two Stones, a fictional retelling of his love story. That same year, over her father’s objections, he and Olga married. At the same time he first signed himself “Sholem Aleichem”: the everyday Yiddish greeting, literally “Peace be with you!”. Rabinovich wanted Yiddish literature to stand alongside literature in Hebrew. In 1888 he founded the yearbook Di yidishe folksbibliotek and gathered the best writers in it, among them Mendele Moykher Sforim and Isaac Leib Peretz.

## Tevye, Yehupets and Boiberik

The first Tevye story came out in 1894. Tevye sells his milk to rich summer visitors in Boiberik, outside Yehupets. Sholem Aleichem invented these names for real places: Kyiv became Yehupets, Boiarka became Boiberik, Odesa became Chornomorsk, and Russia was “Aunt Reyzl”. Most of his stories are set in Ukraine, with Ukrainian landscapes, Ukrainian words and folklore. He loved Ukrainian songs himself and kept [Shevchenko](/en/people/taras-shevchenko/)’s Kobzar in his library. His heroes are poor “little people” who keep their humour and their faith in justice even in hard times.

## From Kyiv to New York

In 1887 Rabinovich settled in Kyiv, played the stock exchange and within a few years went bankrupt. In 1903 he put together a collection called Hilf (“Help”) for the victims of the Kishinev pogrom and invited Tolstoy, Chekhov, Korolenko and Gorky to contribute. After the pogrom against Jews in Kyiv in October 1905 the family moved to Lviv, and in 1906 the writer went to New York for the first time. He travelled widely in Europe, reading to his audiences. In 1908, for his 25th anniversary as a writer, a jubilee committee in Warsaw bought back the rights to all his works from his publishers and handed them to him. That same year he fell ill with tuberculosis. In December 1914 Sholem Aleichem moved to New York for good. There he finished Motl and worked on From the Fair, a novel about his childhood. He died there on 13 May 1916. Hundreds of thousands of people came to his funeral. The New York Times printed his will, and it was read into the US Congressional Record. In it he asked his friends to mark the anniversary of his death by reading one of his merriest stories, and ever since, readers around the world have marked the day with readings from his works. He himself had wished to be buried in Kyiv, next to his father.

## Why it matters today

Sholem Aleichem brought Ukraine’s small towns, Kyiv and Boiarka into world literature, under the names Kasrilevke, Yehupets and Boiberik. Through his books, translated into 64 languages, and through Fiddler on the Roof, the world still sees these places and the people who lived in them. He showed that Yiddish could stand alongside Hebrew as a language of great literature, and that humour helps people keep their dignity even in poverty. His books are a reminder that the history of Ukraine is also the history of its Jewish towns. And when [Bohdan Stupka](/en/people/bohdan-stupka/) played Tevye about 400 times in Kyiv, it showed that this legacy is still alive where it was born.
