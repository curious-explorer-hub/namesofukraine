## Why would a French king look for a bride in Kyiv?

King Henry I of France had lost his wife and still had no heir. So he sent an embassy all the way to Rus’, to Grand Prince Yaroslav the Wise of Kyiv, and the envoys arranged his marriage to Yaroslav’s daughter Anna. On 19 May 1051 (the date proposed by the scholar Marcel Prou), in the cathedral of Reims, Anna married Henry and was crowned Queen of France.

Nobody knows exactly when she was born, and she was probably born in Kyiv. The couple had three sons. The eldest was named Philip, a name hardly ever given in France at the time. It was probably chosen in honor of the Apostle Philip, who was said to have converted Scythia to Christianity, and in the Middle Ages Scythia was identified with Rus’. Philip went on to become King of France himself.

## “Ana Ryina”: a queen on the council

These Cyrillic letters appear on a royal charter of 1063, and they are believed to be Anna’s own signature. They spell out “Queen Anna” in Slavic letters: the Latin Anna Regina or the Old French Anna Reine. It is the only known signature of a Capetian queen on parchment, and the charter is kept at the National Library of France. Twenty-eight documents issued in her name or with her participation are known, and they show that she regularly sat in on the royal council. In 1059 Pope Nicholas II wrote to her, praising her piety, her generosity to the poor and her defense of the oppressed.

After Henry died in 1060, Count Baldwin of Flanders became the official regent for the young Philip, but in practice Anna ruled together with her son. Philip himself wrote in a charter of 1061 that “as a very small child” he took up the kingdom “together with my mother”.

Soon Anna became the de facto, but not official, wife of Count Raoul of Valois, one of the most powerful lords of northern France. Historians suggest the union protected the queen and her young sons. Between 1065 and 1069 Anna founded (or restored) the Monastery of Saint Vincent in Senlis. She died between 1075 and 1079. On his deathbed her grandson, King Louis VI, gave the Abbey of Saint-Denis the “most precious jacinth” (a gemstone) of his grandmother, “daughter of the king of the Rus’”, and ordered it set into the relic of the Crown of Thorns.

The popular story linking Anna to the Reims Gospel, though, is a myth, historians say.

## Why it matters today

Anna’s story shows that 11th-century Kyiv was part of the political life of medieval Europe: a French king sent envoys all the way here for a bride. The Kyivan prince’s daughter became more than a king’s wife: a queen with a say in affairs of state and the mother of a king. That is why she stands today as a symbol of Ukraine’s old ties with Europe, a reminder that Ukraine’s place among European nations goes back almost a thousand years. The first monument to Anna in France was unveiled in Senlis in 2005 (by the sculptors Valentyn and Mykola Znoba), and in 2018 Kyiv gave a bronze statue of her to its sister city of Toulouse.
