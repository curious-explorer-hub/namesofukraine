## A Ukrainian found abroad

Can you feel Ukrainian far away from Ukraine? Oleh Skrypka says that is exactly what happened to him: he first felt Ukrainian abroad. In 1990 his band Vopli Vidopliassova toured France and Switzerland and was written up in Le Monde, and from 1991 to 1996 the musicians lived and worked in France.

## From the Polytechnic to the rock club

Oleh Skrypka was born in 1964 in Tajikistan, to a family of Ukrainian settlers: his father was a doctor and his mother a kindergarten teacher. He spent his childhood in russia. In 1986 he graduated from the radio engineering faculty of the Kyiv Polytechnic Institute, and that same year the band Vopli Vidopliassova was formed in Kyiv. Skrypka sang and played button accordion, guitar and trumpet. In 1987 the band made its debut at the Kyiv Rock Club, won the Rock Parade festival with the song “Tantsi” (Dances) and was voted band of the year by listeners.

## “Vesna” and the return

In 1996 the musicians came back to Ukraine. In 1997 the video for “Vesna” (Spring) came out; the song became a smash hit and marked the band’s return to the Ukrainian stage. That same year came the show “Ve.Ve. and the Army for Eternal Spring!” with the Song and Dance Ensemble of the Armed Forces of Ukraine. The albums Kraina Mrii (1997), Liubov (1998), Mamai (2001) and Faino (2002) followed.

## The Kraina Mrii festival

In 2004 Skrypka organized the first Kraina Mrii ethnic festival, named like the band’s 1997 album. The festival works with folk traditions and invites visitors not just to listen but to join in. Skrypka also started the Rock-Sich rock festival. In 2005 he turned down the title of Honoured Artist of Ukraine, awarded to him for Independence Day; he said it was a message of protest to society. From 2012 Skrypka was a coach on the TV talent show The Voice of Ukraine, and later on The Voice Kids.

## Why it matters today

Oleh Skrypka is called a legend of Ukrainian ethno-rock: in his songs and at Kraina Mrii, folk tradition sounds modern. He showed that tradition doesn’t belong in a museum: it can be sung on a rock stage, and at a festival people can join in rather than just listen. His path shows that you can find your Ukrainian self even if you were born in Tajikistan and grew up in russia. After the full-scale invasion began, his family stayed in Kyiv, and in 2024 he turned his jubilee concert into a collection “for Victory.” Anyone looking for their roots today can start with a song.
