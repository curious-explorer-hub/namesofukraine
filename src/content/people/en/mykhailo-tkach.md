## A student from Dnipro

Mykhailo Tkach comes from Dnipro. He studied at the Oles Honchar Dnipro National University, and that is where he realised journalism was his calling. While still a student he worked at the local Channel 9, and after graduating he moved to Kyiv to join the 1+1 TV channel.

## Schemes

In 2015 Tkach joined Schemes: Corruption in Detail, an investigative programme made jointly by Radio Liberty and Ukraine’s public broadcaster UA:Pershyi. Until 2021 he was its journalist and presenter, and he put out dozens of investigations into the country’s most senior officials. In 2020 Tkach found a hole in his kitchen ceiling leading to a shared attic; experts thought it was most likely an attempt to install covert surveillance. That same year Ukrainska Pravda named him journalist of the year.

## Ukrainska Pravda

In 2021 Tkach became head of the investigations desk at Ukrainska Pravda, the outlet founded by [Georgiy Gongadze](/en/people/heorhii-honhadze/). During the full-scale war he has investigated the routes of trade with Russia, officials who went on holiday abroad while travel was restricted, and the financial scandals of Ukrainian millionaires across Europe. He has also exposed corruption in government contracts and among defence suppliers, reported on Operation Midas, and uncovered covert Russian influence in Western countries.

## Recognition

Tkach was awarded the Order For Merit, 3rd class. Three times in a row he topped the Journalists of the Year ranking compiled by the Institute of Mass Information. In 2026 he received the Georgiy Gongadze Prize, which is given not for a single story but for a professional career and loyalty to the values of journalism. And on 29 September 2026 in Oslo he received a Free Media Award from the Fritt Ord and ZEIT Stiftung foundations for his dauntless and consistent investigative journalism in wartime.

## Why it matters today

Investigative journalism is one way a society keeps watch over those in power, and Mykhailo Tkach’s work shows why it matters. Even in wartime, questions to officials are not a luxury: a country defending itself needs to know whether the people who make the rules follow them. This work can start with a student job at a local channel, as his did in Dnipro. Anyone who asks the powerful awkward questions helps keep a country honest.
