## “That dog’s language is my language!”

Olena Shovheniva was born in 1906 in the village of Ilyinskoye near Moscow, into the family of the hydraulic engineer Ivan Shovheniv (other sources give 1907 and Saint Petersburg). In 1918 the family moved to Kyiv. In 1920 her father left for exile with the UNR government, while 13-year-old Olena stayed in Kyiv with her mother and worked as a messenger at the Kyiv Polytechnic Institute, where he had recently taught: she washed floors, delivered notices and sawed firewood. In 1922 the family was reunited in Poděbrady in Czechoslovakia. Olena grew up in a russian-speaking environment, and as she wrote in 1928, she had known Ukrainian only “very recently”.

In 1934, at a party in Prague, someone began mocking the Ukrainian language. By her own account, printed in Vistnyk, Olena stood up, banged her fist on the table and shouted: “You boors! That dog’s language is my language! The language of my father and my mother! And I don’t want to know you any more!” From then on, she wrote, she spoke only Ukrainian.

## Tenderness as strength

Unlike many stereotypes of the time about women in literature, Olena Teliha was remarkably energetic, modern and full of life. She loved dancing and sport and dressed elegantly. In her poems and essays she wrote about the role of women in the life of the nation: she set her views against those of Western feminists and saw a woman’s mission in “tenderness” and in keeping up the fighting spirit of men.

Her verse is known for its subtle lyricism, love of life and the theme of heroic self-sacrifice. In her essays she condemned the spirit of slavish submission.

## A poet of Vistnyk

In 1923–1929 Olena studied at the Ukrainian Higher Pedagogical Institute in Prague, and in 1926 she married Mykhailo Teliha. In 1929–1939 the Telihas lived in Warsaw, where she taught at a Ukrainian school. From 1933 she published poems and essays in [Dmytro Dontsov](/en/people/dmytro-dontsov/)’s journal Vistnyk. In December 1939 Teliha joined the OUN and in Cracow worked with Oleh Olzhych in its cultural section, heading the Zarevo arts society.

## Kyiv, 1941

She arrived in Nazi-occupied Kyiv with the OUN expeditionary groups on 22 October 1941. There Teliha headed the Union of Ukrainian Writers and edited Litavry, the literary and arts supplement to the newspaper Ukrainske Slovo. She saved the poet Yakiv Halperin, who was of Jewish origin, from arrest and execution: he was given papers under a new surname, Yakiv Halych, and a job at the Litavry office. Teliha also set up a food station. Despite hunger and cold, she arrived at the Union on Trokhsviatytelska Street at exactly 9 every morning, “neatly combed, elegantly dressed, shivering with cold, her fingers blue,” as a comrade recalled.

When the occupation authorities closed Ukrainske Slovo and replaced it with the loyal Nove Ukrainske Slovo, Teliha refused to cooperate. When mass arrests of nationalists began, she did not obey an order from the OUN leadership to leave Kyiv. “I won’t go into emigration from Kyiv again! I can’t…” she told a friend.

## Her husband followed her to her execution

On 9 February 1942 the Gestapo arrested her at the Union’s premises. Her husband Mykhailo Teliha, a Kuban Cossack and bandura player who had never left her side since they met in Prague in 1924, was not a member of the Union. Even so, he voluntarily joined the arrested writers. On the wall of her cell at 33 Korolenka Street Olena scratched a trident and the words “Here sat, and from here goes to her execution, Olena Teliha.”

The Nazis executed Olena and Mykhailo Teliha at Babyn Yar. The exact date is unknown: it is usually given as 21 February 1942, but also as 22 or 13 February. Her poems were published as a book only after her death, in the collection The Soul on Guard (1946).

## Debates and assessments

Teliha belonged to the OUN and published in Vistnyk, edited by Dontsov, the chief ideologue of Ukrainian integral nationalism. Critics of her commemoration, including the Ukrainian Jewish Committee, say the newspaper she worked with was anti-Jewish. In 2017 a monument to Teliha was unveiled at Babyn Yar, where the Nazis also shot tens of thousands of Jews, despite the committee’s objections. Supporters of her commemoration stress that she refused to cooperate with the occupiers and was killed by the Nazis. The assessment of her role in occupied Kyiv remains a subject of debate.

## Why it matters today

Olena Teliha’s story shows that you can claim your language as an adult: a young woman raised among russian speakers stood up one evening in Prague, called Ukrainian her own, and spoke nothing else from then on. In her poems and essays she wrote about the role of women in the life of the nation. In occupied Kyiv she refused to cooperate with the occupiers. Her line “Victory is given only to those who could laugh even in pain” still gives strength to anyone going through hard times.
