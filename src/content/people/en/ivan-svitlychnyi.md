## The flat on Umanska Street

A small, modest flat in Kyiv, at 35 Umanska Street. In the early 1960s it was a second home to [Vasyl Stus](/en/people/vasyl-stus/) and [Vasyl Symonenko](/en/people/vasyl-symonenko/), [Ivan Dziuba](/en/people/ivan-dziuba/) and [Ivan Drach](/en/people/ivan-drach/), [Alla Horska](/en/people/alla-horska/), [Lina Kostenko](/en/people/lina-kostenko/) and [Mykola Vinhranovskyi](/en/people/mykola-vinhranovskyi/), [Yevhen Sverstiuk](/en/people/yevhen-sverstiuk/) and [Mykhailyna Kotsiubynska](/en/people/mykhailyna-kotsiubynska/). The hosts, Ivan and Leonida Svitlychnyi, offered their guests not just talk but a piece of bread, and often a bed for the night. The flat became an unofficial Club of Creative Youth. Ivan recorded young poets on tape, which is how Symonenko’s voice reading his poem “The Thief” survives.

## Despite the war

Ivan Svitlychnyi was born in 1929 in the village of Polovynkyne in the Luhansk region (now Tolokivka). In 1943, during the war, the teenager tried to blow up Nazi vehicles and lost the fingers of both hands. Even so, he learned to write, finished school with a gold medal and went on to Kharkiv University. In Kyiv he worked at academic institutes and on the staff of literary journals, wrote criticism and translated Baudelaire, Béranger and La Fontaine.

## Two arrests

Svitlychnyi spread samvydav: texts retyped and passed from hand to hand to get around the censors. On 30 August 1965 he was arrested, among other things for sending Symonenko’s writings abroad. He was released eight months later, but he could never again find work in his field. Stus wrote the poem “I cannot without Ivan’s smile” under the impression of this arrest.

On 12 January 1972 Svitlychnyi was arrested again, for “anti-Soviet agitation and propaganda”. The sentence: 7 years in strict-regime camps and 5 years of exile. In the camps of the Perm region he was one of the leaders of the prisoners’ resistance, and in exile in the village of Ust-Kan in the Altai he worked as a night watchman and bookbinder. In 1978–1981 he translated The Tale of Ihor’s Campaign. In 1981 he had a stroke, and in 1983 he came back to Kyiv gravely ill.

## The light that stayed

In 1990 his first and only collection of poems published in his lifetime came out, A Heart for Bullets and Rhymes, with a foreword by Dziuba. Ivan Svitlychnyi died in Kyiv on 25 October 1992. His sister Nadiia Svitlychna tried to finish the dictionary of synonyms he had begun. In 1994 he was posthumously awarded the Shevchenko Prize. As those who knew him recalled, much of what we now call the Sixtiers movement gathered around his doorstep on Umanska Street.
