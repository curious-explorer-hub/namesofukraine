## Print runs second only to Shevchenko

In the 1920s, 23 of Ostap Vyshnia’s books went through 42 editions. Only Shevchenko’s works had a bigger total print run. Behind the pen name was a medical assistant, Pavlo Hubenko. He was born on the Chechva farmstead near Hrun in the Poltava region (now Sumy Oblast), the second of seventeen children in a peasant family. He took the first name of his pen name from Ostap, Taras Bulba’s son in the beloved story by his fellow countryman [Mykola Hohol](/en/people/mykola-hohol/), and Vyshnia, “cherry”, because he loved the cherry trees that grew by every house in the Poltava region. At school in Zinkiv his classmate was [Mykola Zerov](/en/people/mykola-zerov/), later a scholar of the classics, but their paths split: “He went for Rome, I went for Shenheriivka,” Vyshnia joked. He went on to train at the Kyiv military medical assistants’ school and worked at the South-Western Railway hospital in Kyiv.

## From medic to “smiles”

In 1917 he enrolled in the history and philology faculty of Kyiv University, though he never graduated. Those days, he said, he was “building Ukraine”, running back and forth between the Central Rada and the university. He first went into print in Kamianets-Podilskyi in 1919, when the government of the Ukrainian People’s Republic was based there, signing himself P. Hrunskyi. Under the Ukrainian People’s Republic he headed the medical and sanitary directorate of the Ministry of Railways: all the railway hospitals were under him, packed with typhus-stricken soldiers of the UNR Army and the Ukrainian Galician Army. For that service the Bolshevik security service jailed him in 1920; the next year Vasyl Blakytnyi, editor of the newspaper Visti VUTsVK, helped get him out.

In 1921 he joined Visti as a translator, until it struck him: “Why am I translating when I could be writing feuilletons!” From then on Kharkiv newspapers and magazines ran his feuilletons and “smiles”, the humorous genre he started and named, now signed Ostap Vyshnia: about the village, language, Ukrainization and russian great-power chauvinism. His language, he wrote, was polished by hard work and by the guidance of [Ahatanhel Krymskyi](/en/people/ahatanhel-krymskyi/) and Modest Levytskyi, whom he had the luck to work with. With [Maik Yohansen](/en/people/maik-yohansen/) and [Mykola Khvylovy](/en/people/mykola-khvylovyi/) he wrote the revue Hello, on Wave 477!, staged by the Berezil theater in 1929.

## Ten years in the North

From the late 1920s official critics hounded the writer. On 25 December 1933 he was groundlessly arrested and charged with nationalism and with plotting to kill the Party leader Pavel Postyshev. Under interrogation he answered with a joke: why not also charge him with an attempt on the honor of Clara Zetkin? On 23 February 1934 he was sentenced to death, but in March the sentence was commuted to ten years in labor camps. He served his term in Ukhta (now in the Komi Republic).

In 1937 he was marched on foot through the snow back to the camp settlement of Chibyu, in effect to be shot. On the way he came down with pneumonia, and by the time he recovered the mass shootings had stopped. Another version says the order for his execution was simply lost.

For years [Oleksandr Dovzhenko](/en/people/oleksandr-dovzhenko/), Mykola Bazhan and Yurii Yanovskyi lobbied for his release; in the end it was won through Nikita Khrushchev. Released on 8 October 1943, he lived in Kyiv from 1944 and wrote again. On 26 February 1944 the newspaper Radianska Ukraina printed his “smile” The Anti-Aircraft Gun, which spread along the front lines. In those years he also finished his Hunting Smiles cycle, about forests, rivers and people out in nature. He loved to go hunting with [Maksym Rylskyi](/en/people/maksym-rylskyi/), though, as people recalled, sometimes he wouldn’t shoot: a duck that beautiful was better let go. Some of his later works, including pamphlets against “Ukrainian bourgeois nationalists”, followed Party guidelines. He was rehabilitated in 1955 and died in Kyiv in 1956.

## Why it matters today

Ostap Vyshnia proved that humor in Ukrainian could win a mass readership: in the 1920s only Shevchenko’s books outsold his. His “smiles” talked with readers about language and Ukrainization and mocked russian great-power chauvinism. The Soviet regime took ten years of his life, and after the camps he went back to humor. His books still show that laughter in Ukrainian can be both comfort and sharp satire.
