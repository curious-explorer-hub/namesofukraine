## Prince Volodymyr with a hetman’s face

Did you know that Hetman Mazepa once “played” Prince Volodymyr? In 1705 a young teacher at the Kyiv-Mohyla Academy, Feofan Prokopovych, wrote the tragicomedy Vladimir, dedicated it to Hetman [Ivan Mazepa](/en/people/ivan-mazepa/) and portrayed him as Grand Prince [Volodymyr](/en/people/volodymyr-velykyi/). After Poltava the same author would call Mazepa “the new Judas”.

## A Kyiv scholar

He was born in Kyiv in 1681 (his secular name was Eleazar). He graduated from the Kyiv-Mohyla Academy in 1696 and went on to study in Lithuania, Poland and at the Greek College of St Athanasius in Rome. In 1704 he came back to Kyiv to teach poetics, rhetoric, philosophy and theology, and from 1711 to 1716 he was rector of the academy. His poetics textbook drew on classical models, and his rhetoric course argued that content matters more than ornament. In philosophy he introduced students to the ideas of Descartes, Bacon and Locke.

## After Poltava

Prokopovych praised Mazepa in his sermons and called Kyiv a second Jerusalem. But after the hetman’s defeat in 1709 he denounced him and swore complete loyalty to Peter I. From then on his career soared: the tsar called him to Saint Petersburg as preacher and adviser, he was consecrated Bishop of Pskov in 1718, and in 1725 he became Archbishop of Novgorod.

## A church under the tsar

In 1721, on Peter I’s orders, Prokopovych wrote the Spiritual Regulation. The statute abolished the patriarchate and handed the running of the church to a Synod under the tsar; Prokopovych became its vice-president. The main provisions stayed in force until 1917. After Peter’s death, opponents among the clergy and the boyars pushed him out of influence. Prokopovych died in Saint Petersburg in 1736.

## Debates and assessments

Prokopovych is called one of the leading theorists of Russian autocracy: under his Spiritual Regulation the church became part of the state bureaucracy. He is also criticized for changing sides: after 1709 he joined the campaign against Mazepa, whom he had once glorified, and called the hetman “the new Judas”. At the same time, scholars recognize his contribution to learning: he brought new ideas of European philosophy and natural science to the Kyiv-Mohyla Academy, and his legacy, as the historian Anna Makhinko writes, belongs to both Ukrainian and Russian culture.

## Why it matters today

Prokopovych is one of the Kyiv-Mohyla graduates who shaped the church, education and government of the whole Russian Empire. His Kyiv works, Vladimir and his books on poetics and rhetoric, remain part of the history of Ukrainian literature, and the Spiritual Regulation governed church life in the Russian Empire for almost 200 years.
