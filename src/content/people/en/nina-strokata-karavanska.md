## A scientist behind barbed wire

Did you know that in 1974 the American Society for Microbiology admitted a scientist who was serving time in a Soviet camp? Her American colleagues demanded that Nina Strokata be freed. She had been locked up for refusing to turn her back on her husband and on Ukraine.

## A doctor from Odesa

Nina Strokata was born in Odesa in 1926. She graduated from the Odesa Medical Institute, worked as a doctor in Tatarbunary, then returned to the institute as a microbiologist and immunologist. She wrote more than 20 scientific papers and spoke five languages.

## A political prisoner's wife

In 1961 Nina married [Sviatoslav Karavanskyi](/en/people/sviatoslav-karavanskyi/), who had just come back from the camps. In 1965 he was arrested again, and their flat in Odesa became a place where people read samizdat and spoke Ukrainian. She was pressed to disown her husband and refused. In 1966, in despair, she wrote to Brezhnev: if they would not stop tormenting her husband, they should rather shoot him. In 1971 she was fired from the institute.

## The first committee in her defence

In December 1971 Strokata was arrested for spreading samizdat, including the Ukrainian Herald. In Lviv, on [Viacheslav Chornovil](/en/people/viacheslav-chornovil/)'s initiative, a Public Committee in her defence was formed, and [Vasyl Stus](/en/people/vasyl-stus/) joined it. It is considered the first openly working human rights organisation in Soviet Ukraine. The court gave Strokata four years in a strict-regime camp.

## Mordovia and Tarusa

In the camp in Mordovia she went on hunger strike with [Iryna Kalynets](/en/people/iryna-kalynets/) and other prisoners, though she was seriously ill. She was released at the end of 1975 but barred from Ukraine, and she renounced her Soviet citizenship. She had to live under police supervision in the small town of Tarusa.

## From there to the Helsinki Group

On 9 November 1976 Strokata became one of the ten founders of the Ukrainian Helsinki Group, alongside [Mykola Rudenko](/en/people/mykola-rudenko/) and [Oksana Meshko](/en/people/oksana-meshko/). She helped draft and signed almost all of the group's documents.

## A voice from across the ocean

In 1979, after Karavanskyi was released, the couple were stripped of Soviet citizenship, and in December they arrived in the United States. In Texas Strokata told the world about Ukrainian political prisoners, women above all, published books about them and raised help for their families. She died in 1998.

## Why it matters today

Nina Strokata showed that loyalty to someone you love can itself be resistance: she chose her imprisoned husband over a career and paid for it with her freedom. Her arrest gave rise to Ukraine's first open human rights committee, and later she helped found the Helsinki Group, which told the world how Ukrainians were persecuted. Her story shows that human rights are defended not only by politicians but by doctors and scientists too. And the solidarity of colleagues across the ocean is a reminder that the truth about injustice crosses any border.
