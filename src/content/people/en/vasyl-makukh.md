## A village boy who joined the insurgents

Vasyl Makukh was born in 1927 in the village of Kariv in the Lviv region, into a peasant family. In late 1944, at seventeen, he joined the Ukrainian Insurgent Army (UPA) and served as a scout. In February 1946 he was wounded in battle and captured.

A Soviet court gave him ten years in the camps and five years of exile. He served them in Mordovia and Siberia and was released in 1956.

## A stranger in his own country

Former insurgents weren’t allowed back home to Galicia. Makukh settled in Dnipropetrovsk, worked as a fitter, married and raised a daughter and a son. He wanted to study and got into university, but he was expelled as soon as his years in the camps came to light.

At home the family spoke Ukrainian, which was already unusual in the city. In 1968 one piece of news shook him: in August Soviet troops rolled into Czechoslovakia to crush the Prague Spring. He told his wife he was ready to give himself for a free Ukraine and for his children’s future.

## A protest on Khreshchatyk

On 5 November 1968, two days before the main Soviet holiday, Vasyl Makukh came to Khreshchatyk in Kyiv, near the Bessarabsky Market, and set himself on fire. He ran towards what is now Independence Square, shouting slogans against the colonisers and the occupiers of Czechoslovakia, and “Long live a free Ukraine!” He died in hospital the next day. He was 40.

It was the first protest of its kind in Ukraine. Soviet newspapers said nothing, but the news reached foreign radio stations. The KGB questioned Makukh’s wife for three months and she lost her job; his funeral in Dnipropetrovsk took place under KGB watch.

## Why it matters today

Two and a half months later, in January 1969, the student Jan Palach made the same protest in Prague, and the whole world knows his name. Makukh’s stayed almost unknown for a long time, even in Ukraine. In 1978 [Oleksa Hirnyk](/en/people/oleksa-hirnyk/) repeated his protest in Kaniv.

Today Dnipro has a Vasyl Makukh Street, and since 2017 a memorial plaque on Khreshchatyk has marked the place where a man gave his life calling for a free Ukraine.
