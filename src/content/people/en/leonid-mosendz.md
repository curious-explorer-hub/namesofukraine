## The patriot who changed his mind

Leonid Mosendz admitted that as a young man he had been a russian patriot. Then, he wrote, "within a month I was a Ukrainian nationalist". He was born in 1897 in Mohyliv-Podilskyi into a russian-speaking family; his father managed the estates of a Polish landowner and died when the boy was seven. His mother was a forester's daughter from Volhynia, and Mosendz later took her maiden name, Liaskovets, as his pen name. Leonid graduated from the teachers' seminary in Vinnytsia, and in the First World War he fought in the russian army on the Romanian front.

## A teacher in the UNR army

After the war Mosendz taught physics and natural science in the small town of Hnivan on the Southern Buh, and served as secretary and librarian of the local Prosvita society. He was an officer in the army of the Ukrainian People's Republic (UNR), survived typhus, and in 1920 retreated with the army into Poland. In Lutsk he was arrested as an active member of Prosvita and sent to a camp for interned soldiers in Kalisz, where he caught tuberculosis.

## The chemist from Poděbrady

In 1922 Mosendz moved to Czechoslovakia, to Poděbrady, home of the Ukrainian Husbandry Academy. His Ukrainian school papers were not recognised, so at 25 the former teacher sat his secondary-school exams again. In 1928 he qualified as an engineer and worked in the laboratories of the academy and of the polytechnic in Brno. He invented a device for the clamps that hold burettes, the glass tubes chemists use to measure out liquids precisely. A Prague firm bought the rights to make and sell it, and the Brno laboratory used it. In 1931 he earned his doctorate, with a dissertation on how to measure moisture in coal. From 1933 he worked in a wine laboratory in Bratislava, and in 1937 he went to teach in Transcarpathia. When Hungary occupied the region in 1939, he returned to Bratislava and worked as a chemist at a wine research institute until the end of the war.

## A poet of the Vistnyk

Mosendz published poems, stories and essays in the Lviv journal Literaturno-naukovyi vistnyk and its successor Vistnyk, both edited by [Dmytro Dontsov](/en/people/dmytro-dontsov/). With [Yevhen Malaniuk](/en/people/yevhen-malaniuk/) and [Oleh Olzhych](/en/people/oleh-olzhych/) he is counted in the "Vistnyk quadriga", the core of the Prague school of émigré poets. His great themes were the noble, strong-willed individual and the Ukrainian struggle for freedom. Back in Poděbrady, Mosendz had tutored the academy lecturers' children for their exams free of charge, and one of his pupils was the young [Olena Teliha](/en/people/olena-teliha/). She later dedicated her poem "A Letter" to him. Mosendz translated from several European languages; in 1934 his translation of Rilke's poem about King Charles XII of Sweden riding through Ukraine came out.

## A novel about the last prophet

From the mid-1930s Mosendz worked on a large novel, The Last Prophet, about John the Baptist. It is set in Judea, against its people's struggle against Roman rule. Some scholars read it as a meditation on the choice an occupied nation faces between accommodation and resistance. Others see in it a rethinking of integral nationalism and the author's turn towards a broader humanism. The novel was left unfinished and came out in Toronto in 1960, twelve years after his death.

## The last exile

In 1945, as the Red Army approached Czechoslovakia, Mosendz left for Austria. In Innsbruck he became friends with the poet [Yurii Klen](/en/people/yurii-klen/). In December 1946 an American relief organisation paid for him to enter a sanatorium in the Swiss town of Blonay. The next year he and Klen published a book of parodies, Diabolic Parabolas, under the joint pen name Porfyrii Horotak. Leonid Mosendz died in Blonay on 13 October 1948, after a second operation on his lungs, aged 51. Only two Ukrainians came to his funeral.

## Why it matters today

Mosendz showed that being Ukrainian can be a deliberate choice: a boy from a russian-speaking family went to fight for the UNR and became a Ukrainian writer. Having lost his homeland, he lost neither his profession nor his language: he earned a doctorate in chemistry abroad and at the same time became one of the voices of the Prague school. His Last Prophet tells of a people under foreign rule looking for a way to freedom, and it still asks how to fight for it without losing one's humanity.
