## Who he was

Pavlo Skoropadskyi was born in Wiesbaden, Germany, into a family descended from the old Ukrainian Cossack officer (starshyna) lineage of the Skoropadskyis. He spent his childhood on the family estate of Trostianets in the Poltava region (today the Chernihiv region) and studied at the Page Corps in St. Petersburg. He served in the Russian imperial guard and fought in the Russo-Japanese War and the First World War, earning the Order of St. George, 4th class, and the rank of lieutenant general for his service in battle. Even before the revolution he was interested in Ukrainian antiquities and the history of his family.

## His story

In 1917 Skoropadskyi Ukrainized the 34th Army Corps, turning it into the 1st Ukrainian Corps, and became otaman of the Free Cossacks. At the end of 1917 his troops disarmed Bolshevized units returning from the front and kept them out of Kyiv. Disagreeing with the socialist policies of the Central Rada, especially its land reform, in the spring of 1918 he founded the conservative Ukrainian People’s Hromada.

On 29 April 1918, with the backing of the German command, Skoropadskyi was proclaimed hetman at a congress of landowning farmers. The Ukrainian People’s Republic became the Ukrainian State, the Central Rada was dissolved, and all power was concentrated in the hetman’s hands. His government established diplomatic relations with many countries and founded a number of key institutions: the Ukrainian Academy of Sciences, Ukrainian state universities in Kyiv and Kamianets-Podilskyi, the National Library, the state archives and more than 150 Ukrainian gymnasiums. But the regime did not solve the land and social questions, peasants rose in revolt, and stability rested on German and Austro-Hungarian troops. After the defeat of the Central Powers, on 14 November 1918, the hetman issued the Federation Charter on union with a future non-Bolshevik Russia. On the same day the Ukrainian National Union formed the Directory, which launched an uprising. On 14 December 1918 Skoropadskyi abdicated and left for Germany.

In exile he lived in Wannsee near Berlin and led the hetmanite movement, which united Ukrainian monarchists in Europe and North America. In 1926 the Ukrainian Scientific Institute in Berlin was founded on his initiative. During the Second World War he sought the release of imprisoned OUN leaders from Nazi camps. In April 1945 Skoropadskyi was mortally wounded in a bombing raid on the railway station at Plattling in Bavaria and died in Metten; he was buried in Oberstdorf.

## Debates and assessments

Assessments of the 1918 Hetmanate still differ. Critics, including the Ukrainian democratic and socialist parties of the time, pointed out that Skoropadskyi’s power came from a coup backed by the German army, that it was authoritarian, relied on large landowners, introduced censorship and repression, and brought many Russian figures hostile to Ukrainian independence into the government and army. The Federation Charter of 14 November 1918 became the formal pretext for the anti-hetman uprising.

At the same time, historians acknowledge that the Ukrainian State achieved significant successes in diplomacy, education and culture: the Ukrainian Academy of Sciences, universities, the National Library and a network of Ukrainian gymnasiums all date from that time. The Encyclopedia of the History of Ukraine describes his rule as an attempt to build a temporary one-man government that drew on historical and national traditions and tended toward authoritarianism, and notes that in exile Skoropadskyi abandoned his federalist views.

## Why it matters today

Skoropadskyi’s brief Hetmanate left institutions that still exist: the National Academy of Sciences of Ukraine and the National Library of Ukraine trace their history to 1918. His experience shows how hard it was to build a state amid war, foreign military presence and deep social division.
