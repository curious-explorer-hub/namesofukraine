## A knife in the sun

In the summer of 1961 the weekly Literaturna Ukraina printed a long poem by a young author, A Knife in the Sun. Some readers were shocked by its unusual form and by themes that were meant to be avoided; others were baffled by its difficult language; still others greeted it as a long-awaited revelation. That was how Ivan Drach arrived.

He was born in 1936 in the village of Telizhyntsi in the Kyiv region and studied at Kyiv University. His first collection, Sunflower, came out in 1962. Together with the debuts of [Mykola Vinhranovskyi](/en/people/mykola-vinhranovskyi/) and [Vasyl Symonenko](/en/people/vasyl-symonenko/), it became one of the central events in the literature of those years.

## A poet under attack

The success didn’t last. In 1963 the leadership of the Communist Party of Ukraine publicly condemned Drach; Soviet papers stopped printing his poems, and he was expelled from the university. He turned to screenwriting: A Well for the Thirsty, directed by Yurii Illienko, would come to be seen as one of the high points of Ukrainian poetic cinema.

In 1965 the arrests of the Sixtiers began. At the premiere of [Serhii Paradzhanov](/en/people/serhii-paradzhanov/)’s Shadows of Forgotten Ancestors, Drach stood up with others in support of [Ivan Dziuba](/en/people/ivan-dziuba/), who spoke out against the arrests. In 1966 he went to the trial of those arrested with [Lina Kostenko](/en/people/lina-kostenko/), [Viacheslav Chornovil](/en/people/viacheslav-chornovil/) and Ivan Dziuba, and at a writers’ meeting he and Lina Kostenko tried to defend the convicted. For this he was given a severe reprimand.

## Debates and assessments

Three days after that meeting Drach wrote an explanatory note to the Party committee condemning his own actions. His biography in the Museum of the Dissident Movement of the Kharkiv Human Rights Protection Group calls this the beginning of his capitulation, but notes that he did not cross over to the opponents’ camp. In 1973 he again had to “repent”; in 1976 he received the Shevchenko Prize, and in 1983 the USSR State Prize. Some scholars judge that his compromise with the regime weakened his later poetry. Ivan Dziuba writes that the poet had to manoeuvre to break through to a word of truth. The cultural scholar Oksana Pakhlovska recalls that Drach himself admitted writing conformist verse to get his books past the censors.

## From poetry to the state

In May 1986 Drach was the first at the Congress of Writers of Ukraine to speak openly about the Holodomor, and in 1988 he wrote the poem The Chornobyl Madonna. In 1989 he became the first leader of the People’s Movement of Ukraine. He then served four terms as a member of parliament, headed the State Committee for Information Policy (2000–2002) and in 2006 was made a Hero of Ukraine. Drach died in Kyiv in 2018 and is buried in his home village.

## Why it matters today

Drach’s works have been translated into dozens of languages, and his name is considered one of the symbols of the fullness of modern Ukrainian culture.
