## The opera he wouldn’t give up

Pyotr Tchaikovsky offered to stage Mykola Lysenko’s opera Taras Bulba. There was one condition: it would be sung in Russian. Lysenko refused. For his operas he accepted only Ukrainian, even though the Ems Ukaz of 1876 banned printing Ukrainian even in the words to sheet music. In the composer’s lifetime only fragments of Taras Bulba were staged; the full opera reached the stage in 1924.

## How a natural scientist became a composer

Lysenko was born in 1842 in the village of Hrynky in the Poltava region, into a noble family of Cossack descent. He had music lessons from the age of five. His mother taught him seven languages, music and good manners, but she never learned Ukrainian herself. When she tried to speak it to please him, he would jokingly ask her to stop tormenting Ukraine. He graduated in natural sciences from Kyiv University, then from the Leipzig Conservatory, and went on to study orchestration with Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov in Saint Petersburg.

All his life Lysenko wrote down folk songs and the melodies of kobzars, and he made hundreds of folk-song arrangements. On that basis he created Ukrainian operas, choral works, art songs and piano music. His opera Christmas Night, after Gogol, staged in Kyiv in 1874, marked the birth of Ukrainian opera theatre. He set texts by [Shevchenko](/en/people/taras-shevchenko/) (over 80 works to poems from Kobzar), [Kotliarevskyi](/en/people/ivan-kotliarevskyi/), [Gogol](/en/people/mykola-hohol/) and [Lesya Ukrainka](/en/people/lesya-ukrainka/). He also helped prepare the Dictionary of the Ukrainian Language and took part in the 1897 census of Kyiv.

In 1904 Lysenko founded a school of music and drama in Kyiv, the first in eastern Ukraine to teach in Ukrainian. His friends raised the money for it for his jubilee: the 4,000 rubles covered only the rent on a building, the instruments were hired, and about 500 people applied to study.

## Watched to the very end

The tsarist authorities regarded Lysenko with suspicion. In 1907 he was arrested for a short time, then released. In 1908 he became head of the Kyiv Ukrainian Club, the first legal Ukrainian civic and political organisation, with literary and music evenings, lectures, a choir and a library. On 2 November 1912 the authorities ordered criminal charges against the club’s leaders, headed by Lysenko, for “anti-state activity” and “Ukrainian nationalism”. Four days later, on 6 November, the composer died of a heart attack.

## Why it matters today

Lysenko proved that Ukrainian music could be classical music. He built operas, choral works and art songs on folk song, and so kept for us what kobzars and villagers sang: “Folklore is life itself.” By turning down Tchaikovsky himself, he showed that your own language is not up for bargaining, even when it is banned. His “Prayer for Ukraine” became the country’s spiritual anthem, and anyone who sings in Ukrainian on stage follows the road he laid.
