## Sentenced for a book about someone else’s novel

When [Oles Honchar](/en/people/oles-honchar/) wrote his novel The Cathedral, Yevhen Sverstiuk answered it with an essay, A Cathedral in Scaffolding. It circulated in samvydav (samizdat), retyped and passed from hand to hand out of the censors’ reach, and in 1970 it came out as a book in Paris. That same year Radio Liberty began broadcasting Sverstiuk, and those broadcasts later ended up in his criminal case. The book itself became the main evidence against him.

## From a Volhynian village to the Club of Creative Youth

Sverstiuk was born in 1928 in the village of Siltse in Volhynia. He graduated from Lviv University, taught school and literature, and worked at a research institute and on journals, and four times he was fired for political reasons. From 1960 he was one of the most active members of the Club of Creative Youth in Kyiv, alongside [Alla Horska](/en/people/alla-horska/), [Ivan Dziuba](/en/people/ivan-dziuba/), [Lina Kostenko](/en/people/lina-kostenko/) and [Vasyl Stus](/en/people/vasyl-stus/). In 1964 he spoke at an evening in memory of [Vasyl Symonenko](/en/people/vasyl-symonenko/). With [Ivan Svitlychnyi](/en/people/ivan-svitlychnyi/) he anonymously wrote On the Trial of Pohruzhalskyi, a pamphlet about the arson at the Central Scientific Library in Kyiv and one of the sharpest samvydav texts of the 1960s. And in 1970 Sverstiuk gave a speech at Alla Horska’s funeral.

## Seven years in camps, five in exile

Sverstiuk was arrested on 14 January 1972. In 1973 he was convicted of “anti-Soviet agitation and propaganda” together with Svitlychnyi: 7 years in strict-regime camps in the Perm region and 5 years of exile in Buryatia. In the camp he joined political prisoners’ hunger strikes and protests, and in exile he worked as a carpenter. He was freed in 1983.

## Words that outlived the censors

Back in Kyiv, Sverstiuk made a living as a carpenter. His articles were printed again only in the late 1980s. In 1989 he founded the newspaper Nasha Vira (Our Faith) and edited it, and in 1993 he became president of the Ukrainian PEN Centre. In 1995 his essay collection Prodigal Sons of Ukraine won him the Shevchenko Prize. Yevhen Sverstiuk died in Kyiv on 1 December 2014. The essays he was once tried for are now published freely in Ukraine, the voice of a generation that refused to stay silent.
