## A city named after an actor

Did you know that one of Ukraine’s regional capitals is named after an actor? In 2016 Kirovohrad was renamed Kropyvnytskyi in honour of Marko Kropyvnytskyi, known as the father of Ukrainian theatre. It was here, in what was then Yelysavethrad, that he gathered the first professional Ukrainian troupe in 1882.

## A clerk who lived for the stage

Marko Kropyvnytskyi was born in 1840 in the village of Bezhbairaky in the Kherson region, now Kropyvnytske in Kirovohrad Oblast. For several years he worked as a minor clerk and gave his free time to amateur shows. He acted in them alongside Ivan Tobilevych, the future playwright [Ivan Karpenko-Karyi](/en/people/ivan-karpenko-karyi/). In 1863 Kropyvnytskyi wrote his first play.

## Banned in the middle of a show

In 1871 he went professional in Odesa: his first role was Stetsko in [Hryhorii Kvitka-Osnovianenko](/en/people/hryhorii-kvitka-osnovianenko/)’s Matchmaking at Honcharivka.

Imagine: it is 1876, in Katerynoslav, and a Ukrainian play is on. Between the third and fourth acts an official walks backstage and announces that such plays are now banned. That is how the actors learned of the Ems Ukaz.

For five years Kropyvnytskyi worked in Russian troupes, mostly in operetta, and all that time he fought for the right to perform in Ukrainian again.

## The Theatre of Coryphaei

When the ban was eased, Kropyvnytskyi put together his own troupe. On 27 October 1882 in Yelysavethrad it performed [Ivan Kotliarevskyi](/en/people/ivan-kotliarevskyi/)’s Natalka from Poltava, and that day is regarded as the birthday of professional Ukrainian theatre. [Mariia Zankovetska](/en/people/mariia-zankovetska/) made her debut in that show. [Mykola Sadovskyi](/en/people/mykola-sadovskyi/), [Panas Saksahanskyi](/en/people/panas-saksahanskyi/) and Ivan Karpenko-Karyi acted there too, and the troupe became known as the Theatre of Coryphaei.

In 1883 [Mykhailo Starytskyi](/en/people/mykhailo-starytskyi/) took over the troupe, while Kropyvnytskyi stayed on as director and actor. The same year the troupe was banned from five provinces for ten years.

## A full house in the imperial capital

In 1886 Kropyvnytskyi took his actors to Saint Petersburg and Moscow. Tickets were expensive, yet the halls filled up, and the takings broke the records of local theatres and touring companies. Kropyvnytskyi was invited to work in Russian art, where a career was there for the taking. He said no.

## From Stetsko to Othello

Kropyvnytskyi wrote 41 plays, among them The Leech and Two Families, and adapted [Taras Shevchenko](/en/people/taras-shevchenko/)’s poem The Captive for the stage. He was at once actor, director, composer and stage designer. He played comedy and drama alike, from Stetsko to Taras Bulba and Othello. And he trained several generations of actors.

## A farm where children took the stage

In 1903 Kropyvnytskyi settled on his Zatyshok farm in the Kharkiv region. There he set up a children’s theatre whose actors were the children of local peasants, and he wrote fairy-tale plays for them, such as Ivasyk Telesyk. For two years the children of Taras Shevchenko’s niece lived at Zatyshok, cared for by the Kropyvnytskyi family.

In 1910 Kropyvnytskyi died suddenly on the way from Mykolaiv to Kharkiv, returning from a tour. He was buried in Kharkiv.

## Why it matters today

Kropyvnytskyi made Ukrainian theatre professional at a time when the empire banned the Ukrainian word on stage. He proved that a Ukrainian play could fill the halls even in the imperial capital, and he chose the Ukrainian stage when he could have built a career on the Russian one. His troupe became a school for the actors who carried Ukrainian theatre through the decades that followed, and his plays are still staged. Anyone who steps onto a Ukrainian stage today carries on the work he began.
