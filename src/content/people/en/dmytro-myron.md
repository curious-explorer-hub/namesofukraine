## Rules from a prison cell

Can you write rules for living while sitting behind bars? Dmytro Myron did exactly that. In a Polish prison he and Zenon Kossak drew up the “44 Rules of Life of a Ukrainian Nationalist”: on a strong will, work for the nation, self-reliance and modesty. Later, as “Maksym Orlyk”, Myron wrote The Idea and Deed of Ukraine, one of the main ideological texts of the Organization of Ukrainian Nationalists (OUN).

## An orphan from Rai

Dmytro Myron was born on 5 November 1911 in the village of Rai near Berezhany, into a poor family. He was orphaned early and brought up by his older sister. At the Berezhany gymnasium he was in Plast, the Ukrainian scouts, and at 15 he joined the underground nationalist youth. He was expelled from school for belonging to the OUN. In 1930 Myron enrolled in law at Lviv University.

## Seven years behind bars

In the OUN Myron worked with young people and co-edited the organization’s first underground publication in Ukraine. In October 1933 the Polish police arrested him, and a court gave him seven years. Among the charges was involvement in [Mykola Lemyk](/en/people/mykola-lemyk/)’s attack at the Soviet consulate in Lviv. More than a hundred hours of interrogation got the investigators nowhere. In the prison at Wronki he and Kossak prepared ideological lectures and the rules. At the Warsaw trial of [Stepan Bandera](/en/people/stepan-bandera/) and other OUN members, Myron refused to give evidence in Polish and was punished for it. He was released in 1938.

## The Idea and Deed

After his release Myron became the political officer of the OUN’s regional leadership. In Kraków he wrote The Idea and Deed of Ukraine, much of it from memory, because the first manuscript had to be destroyed. In 1940 he was among those who set up the OUN’s Revolutionary Leadership under Bandera. That spring Myron crossed the border into the western Ukrainian lands occupied by the USSR and for a time led the underground there.

## Underground in occupied Kyiv

In the summer of 1941 Myron headed east with the OUN’s marching groups. At the end of August the Germans arrested some of his comrades, but he escaped. That autumn he became the OUN’s regional leader for central and eastern Ukraine and drew young people and intellectuals into the underground. On 25 July 1942 (some sources give the 24th) the Germans seized him in Kyiv. Myron tried to break free and was shot dead at the corner of what are now Bohdan Khmelnytskyi and Lysenko streets. He was 30.

## Debates and assessments

Myron was one of the ideologues of integral nationalism, which put the nation above the individual. Some historians call the OUN’s ideology of the 1930s authoritarian, even totalitarian: it took the cult of the leader and a strict hierarchy from Italian fascism. They also note that the OUN rejected Nazi racism, and that the nationalism of a stateless people aimed above all at liberation. In The Idea and Deed Myron warned against blindly copying fascism and Nazism, and instead of party democracy proposed a strong head of state and the representation of working groups. Scholars write that after 1941 the experience of central and eastern Ukraine pushed the OUN to revise its ideology towards democracy. The authorship of the “44 Rules” is debated too: some credit them to Kossak, others see Myron as co-author or even the main author.

## Why it matters today

Myron gave the Ukrainian underground a language for talking about the state, will and duty: his texts taught a generation that went on to fight for independence. He carried the idea of a united Ukrainian state from Galicia to occupied Kyiv and paid for it with his life. His views are still argued over, and that is exactly why they teach us to think: which ideas of that struggle a free, democratic Ukraine takes with it, and which it leaves in the past.
