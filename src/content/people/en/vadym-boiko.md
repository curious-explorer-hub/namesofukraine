## The show they pushed to late night

Did you know that one of the most talked-about youth TV shows of the late 1980s kept being moved to a late slot by the authorities? It was Ukrainian Television's Molodizhna studiia "Hart" (Youth Studio Hart), and one of its faces was Vadym Boiko. He was born on 5 November 1962 in Svitlovodsk in the Kirovohrad region and in 1984 graduated from the journalism faculty of Kyiv University. That same year he joined Hart as an editor, and later became chief editor of youth programmes.

## What had long gone unsaid

In his Hart reports Boiko spoke about things Soviet television had long kept quiet: the Holodomor of 1932–1933, the reburial of [Vasyl Stus](/en/people/vasyl-stus/) in Kyiv, the truth about the Chornobyl disaster. He worked as a freelance correspondent for the Moscow programme Vzglyad, and searched for traces of the legendary library of Yaroslav the Wise.

## An MP at 27

On 18 March 1990 voters in Kremenchuk elected the 27-year-old journalist to parliament, with more than 56% in the second round. In the first convocation Boiko became deputy chair of the Commission on Glasnost and the Mass Media and joined the opposition People's Council (Narodna Rada).

## His death

On 14 February 1992 an explosion and a fire broke out in his Kyiv home on Dovzhenko Street. Vadym Boiko died; he was 29. Prosecutors blamed an exploding TV picture tube. But witnesses saw the door of the home burning, and the forensic examination found that Boiko had died before the fire: there was no soot in his lungs. The remains of a petrol canister were later found there too. The criminal case was opened three times and closed each time as an accident: no one involved was ever identified.

## Debates and assessments

Officially, Vadym Boiko's death is considered an accident. His colleagues, friends and family have said from the first day that he was killed for his work. According to them, before his death he was checking reports that military property and weapons from Soviet troops withdrawn from East Germany were being sold illegally, and was asking where the Communist Party's money had gone after the party was banned. The investigation did not confirm any of this, and who was behind his death is still unknown. Boiko is described as probably the first journalist to die in unexplained circumstances in independent Ukraine, and colleagues compare his fate with that of [Georgiy Gongadze](/en/people/heorhii-honhadze/), who disappeared in 2000.

## Why it matters today

Vadym Boiko was one of the journalists who began telling the truth about the Holodomor and Chornobyl on screen while it was still the Soviet Union. He showed that a journalist can become the voice of voters and stand up for free speech in parliament. His unsolved case is a reminder that free speech in independent Ukraine had a price from the very start, and that the truth about such cases is still worth seeking. Anyone who today asks about what those in power would rather hide carries on his work.
