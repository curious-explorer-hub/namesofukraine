## The prince they had to ask twice

In 1113 Prince Sviatopolk Iziaslavych of Kyiv died, and the townspeople rose up, angry at moneylenders and at profiteering in salt. The town assembly, gathered in St Sophia’s Cathedral, invited Volodymyr Monomakh to take the throne. He refused, perhaps because princes senior to him in the family were still alive. But the uprising went on, and at the second invitation Monomakh agreed. He was 60 years old.

## Grandson of Yaroslav the Wise

Volodymyr was born in 1053. His father, Vsevolod, was a son of [Yaroslav the Wise](/en/people/yaroslav-mudryi/), and his mother was a daughter of the Byzantine emperor Constantine IX Monomachos (her origin is still debated). From her came the name Monomakh. He grew up in Pereiaslav, where his father ruled under Yaroslav’s will. At 12 or 13 Volodymyr was already helping his father in war and politics, and over his life he led more than 80 campaigns.

## The man who gave up Kyiv

When his father died in 1093, Monomakh could have taken Kyiv. Instead he yielded the throne to his cousin Sviatopolk, the senior in the family. The next year he gave Chernihiv to another cousin, Oleh Sviatoslavych, and moved to his father’s Pereiaslav. The chronicler explains that Monomakh did not want the princes to go to war with each other.

## Peace among princes, war with the Cumans

The nomadic Cumans raided the southern lands of Rus’ almost every year, and the Pereiaslav principality, jutting out into the steppe, suffered most. Monomakh tried to unite the princes against them. In 1097, at his initiative, the princes met at Liubech and agreed on a new order of holding lands, and at the congresses of 1100 and 1103 they agreed on joint campaigns. After the campaigns of 1103, 1107, 1109 and 1111 the Cumans were pushed back beyond the Don, and one of their hordes moved all the way to Georgia.

## A law for debtors

Once Grand Prince of Kyiv, Monomakh turned to the causes of the uprising. His Statute, added to the Ruska Pravda law code, capped interest on loans and stopped debtors from being turned into bondsmen. He again gathered power over all the lands of Rus’ into his own hands and was the last prince under whom Rus’ was a single state.

## A letter to his children

Late in life Monomakh wrote the Instruction for My Children: he told the story of his life and campaigns, condemned the princes’ feuds and called for unity. The chronicle also preserves his letter to Oleh Sviatoslavych, written after Monomakh’s son was killed in a war with Oleh.

## Why it matters today

Monomakh died in Kyiv on 19 May 1125 and was buried in St Sophia’s Cathedral. The Kyivan, Smolensk and Suzdal branches of the Rurikid dynasty descend from him. And the Instruction is still read as the voice of the prince himself: a brave warrior and a judicious ruler who told his descendants not to fight one another.
