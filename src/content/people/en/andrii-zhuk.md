## A “grey cardinal” from the Lubny region

Why did the historian Ihor Hyrych call Andrii Zhuk the “grey cardinal” of the movement for Ukrainian independence? Because Zhuk seldom held the top post, yet in Hyrych’s view he was the one who really set the ideas of the groups he worked in. He was born in 1880 in the village of Vovchok (now Vovchyk) near Lubny, into an old Cossack family. He finished only a three-year parish school and taught himself the rest. He worked as a clerk, and at twenty he joined an underground Ukrainian circle in Lubny led by Mykola Porsh. The circle grew into a cell of the Revolutionary Ukrainian Party, the first political party in the Ukrainian lands under russian rule.

## Two prisons and an escape to Lviv

In Kharkiv in 1903 Zhuk organized railway workers and handed out banned Ukrainian books, and was jailed for it. In 1905 he became a founder of the Ukrainian Social Democratic Workers’ Party and its general secretary. In 1906 he was arrested again and held in Lukianivka Prison in Kyiv. In 1907, together with [Volodymyr Vynnychenko](/en/people/volodymyr-vynnychenko/), he was let out on bail, and soon, rather than face trial, he slipped across the border into Austria-Hungary. From then on he lived outside russian-ruled Ukraine: first in Lviv, then part of Austrian Galicia.

## Co-operatives and the dream of independence

In Lviv Zhuk edited the co-operative journals Ekonomist and Samopomich and worked in the Prosvita and Silskyi Hospodar societies. He urged his comrades to put the national question at the top of the party programme and argued for an independent Ukrainian state. For this the Social Democratic leaders expelled him from the party. In 1912 he founded the Ukrainian Information Committee to tell Europe about the Ukrainian cause.

## A union to free Ukraine

When the First World War began in 1914, the Union for the Liberation of Ukraine grew out of that committee. Émigrés from russian-ruled Ukraine founded it in Lviv on 4 August 1914, on Zhuk’s initiative, hoping to free their land from the russian empire with the help of the Central Powers, Austria-Hungary and Germany. Zhuk, Volodymyr Doroshenko and Dmytro Dontsov told Metropolitan [Andrei Sheptytskyi](/en/people/andrei-sheptytskyi/) about the new union, and after reading its platform he fully agreed with it. At the end of August, as russian troops occupied Galicia, the union and Zhuk moved to Vienna. There he ran the union’s Central Bureau, edited its Vistnyk (Herald) and managed its publications. Officially he was only one of its leaders, but he is considered to have been its real head.

The union also worked in camps for prisoners of war from the tsar’s army, among Ukrainians who had fought in the russian ranks. Zhuk put great effort into awakening their sense of nationhood, and the camps are believed to have raised thousands of Ukrainians who knew who they were. At the union’s call, thousands of them volunteered to defend Ukraine, and these volunteers formed two divisions: the Blue Coats in Germany and the Grey Coats in Austria.

Don’t mix it up with the Spilka vyzvolennia Ukrainy, often also translated as Union for the Liberation of Ukraine: that group was invented by the Soviet secret police in 1930 so that [Serhii Yefremov](/en/people/serhii-yefremov/) and other Ukrainian intellectuals could be convicted at a show trial in Kharkiv.

## Diplomat and co-operator

In 1918 Zhuk worked at the embassy of the Ukrainian State in Vienna, and in 1919 he became an adviser to the Foreign Ministry of the Ukrainian People’s Republic. Late in 1919 he joined the Ukrainian People’s Party, which held monarchist views. In early 1920 he resigned, because he disagreed with the alliance that [Symon Petliura](/en/people/symon-petliura/) was making with Poland. For a time he worked as an aide to the head of the Western Ukrainian People’s Republic, [Yevhen Petrushevych](/en/people/yevhen-petrushevych/). In 1930 Zhuk stepped back from active politics and returned to Lviv, then part of Poland. There he headed the co-operative statistics bureau of the Audit Union of Ukrainian Co-operatives, which brought together more than 3,000 co-operatives, and edited the journal Kredytova kooperatsiia. When the Soviet Union occupied Galicia in 1939, Zhuk fled, and from 1940 he lived in Vienna again. He gave his last years to the history of the Ukrainian liberation movement. Andrii Zhuk died in Vienna on 3 September 1968. His papers, among them a diary that spans 60 years, are held at Library and Archives Canada in Ottawa.

## Debates and assessments

The Union for the Liberation of Ukraine counted on Austria-Hungary and Germany defeating the russian empire, so it worked together with the Central Powers. Zhuk himself is said to have tried to keep the public from seeing the union as a tool of the Austrian and German secret services. After 1923 he leaned for a time towards the Soviets, hoping that national communists would build an independent Ukraine. Some scholars see this “Bolshevik orientation” as an attempt to use the rivalry between russia and Poland for the sake of Ukrainian statehood.

## Why it matters today

Even before the First World War, Andrii Zhuk called on Ukrainians to fight for a state of their own, and he gave his whole life to that goal. A boy with three years of village schooling taught himself to become an editor, a politician and a diplomat who told Europe about Ukraine. He never chased high office, yet he set the course for those who held it. His life shows that a country is built not only at the front and in government offices, but also in newsrooms and co-operatives. In 2023 in Lubny, and in 2024 in his home village of Vovchyk, streets that had carried the names of Soviet figures were renamed after him.
