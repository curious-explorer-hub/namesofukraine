## A forward with an engineering degree

Valeriy Lobanovskyi was born in 1939 in Kyiv. He finished Kyiv School No. 319 with a silver medal, and the school now bears his name. He played as a forward for Dynamo Kyiv: in 1961 Dynamo became the first Soviet champion from outside Moscow, and Lobanovskyi was part of that team. Later he played for Chornomorets Odesa and Shakhtar Donetsk. Alongside football he studied engineering: he started at the Kyiv Polytechnic Institute and graduated from the Odesa one in 1965. That training later reshaped how he coached.

## Football by numbers

Lobanovskyi began coaching at 29, in 1968, with Dnipro in Dnipropetrovsk. In 1973 he took over Dynamo, which he led, with breaks, until 2002. Together with the scientist Anatolii Zelentsov he put the team's preparation on a scientific footing. They measured players' physical performance, analyzed match statistics and built training around precise calculations. Even so, he had his superstitions: he never let players take the field wearing number 13, and he tried not to step on running-track lines or cracks in the asphalt.

The trophy cabinet shows the result. Under him Dynamo won eight Soviet league titles, six Soviet Cups and the Cup Winners' Cup twice, in 1975 and 1986, and its players [Oleh Blokhin](/en/people/oleh-blokhin/) and Ihor Belanov won the Ballon d'Or. In the 1990s Lobanovskyi came back to the club and raised a new generation, including [Andriy Shevchenko](/en/people/andrii-shevchenko/) and Serhii Rebrov. In 1999 Dynamo beat the holders, Real Madrid, in the Champions League quarter-final and reached the semi-final. With the USSR team Lobanovskyi won bronze at the 1976 Olympic Games and silver at Euro 1988, and he also coached the national teams of the UAE, Kuwait and Ukraine.

In 2002 he suffered a stroke during a match in Zaporizhzhia and died a few days later. He was posthumously made a Hero of Ukraine.

## Why it matters today

Lobanovskyi showed that a Ukrainian club could beat Europe’s best through system, hard work and science. He measured players and counted match statistics when hardly anyone did. His school raised more than one generation of players who made Kyiv’s name in Europe. His example shows that winning takes more than talent: it also takes knowledge and precise calculation, and not only in football. Kyiv’s Dynamo Stadium bears his name.
