## World No. 100 in the final

In spring 1999, in Monte Carlo, Andrii Medvediev (often spelled Andrei Medvedev) was seriously thinking of quitting. It was Andre Agassi who talked him out of it, picking apart his game and giving him a few tips. Medvediev arrived at Roland-Garros ranked 100th in the world, without two wins in a row all season. Then in Paris he beat world No. 2 Pete Sampras in the second round and Gustavo Kuerten, one of the favourites, in the quarter-finals, and reached the final. Waiting for him there was Agassi. Medvediev took the first two sets 6–1, 6–2. As the third set began, rain stopped play, and after the break Agassi turned the match around and won in five sets.

## The junior from Kyiv

Andrii Medvediev was born on 31 August 1974 in Kyiv. His mother, Svitlana Medvedieva, was a well-known tennis coach, and his first coach was his stepfather, Yurii Cherepov. In 1990 he won the Orange Bowl junior tournament in Miami, and in 1991 he became junior champion at Roland-Garros. He won his first senior title, in Genoa, at 17. In 1993 he reached the Roland-Garros semi-finals, the US Open quarter-finals and the Paris Masters final, broke into the world top 10, and at the end of the season went out to Sampras only in the semi-finals of the ATP World Championship. That summer, aged 18, he also played in the final of the very first tournament in Halle, Germany: it was his only final on grass.

## King of Hamburg

In 1994 Medvediev won two Masters titles on clay, in Monte Carlo and Hamburg, and in May he rose to No. 4 in the world. He was the first European to win two such events in a row, and it was ten years before anyone did it again. In early 1995 he and his older sister Nataliia, also a tennis player, reached the Hopman Cup final in Perth, Australia, where Ukraine lost to Boris Becker and Anke Huber of Germany. That same year he was offered a move to Moscow on generous terms, with a luxury flat and a big salary, and turned it down. He won Hamburg again in 1995 and 1997, and 11 ATP titles in all. From 1993 he played for Ukraine in the Davis Cup: in his first year he won all eight of his matches, and the team moved up a group.

## After the court

Medvediev retired in 2001. From 2004 to 2006 he headed the Ukrainian Tennis Federation, was its honorary president from 2006 to 2013, and in 2013 he became coach of Ukraine's national team. In March 2022, after russia's full-scale invasion, Medvediev, then 47, joined Ukraine's reserve together with fellow players Serhiy Stakhovskyi and Oleksandr Dolhopolov. That June he drove 1,980 kilometres from Kyiv to Halle to step back onto the court where he had played that 1993 final.

## Why it matters today

In the first years of independence, when the world was only getting to know Ukraine, Andrii Medvediev was among the best tennis players on the planet and played for its national team. He proved that a player brought up in Kyiv could win the most prestigious clay-court titles and stand among the world's top four. His 1999 final is a reminder that even the world No. 100 can beat No. 2 if he doesn't give up.
