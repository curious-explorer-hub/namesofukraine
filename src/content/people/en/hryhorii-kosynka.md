## A flower’s name

The writer’s real surname was Strilets. Hryhorii was born in 1899 in the village of Shcherbanivka in the Kyiv region (now Obukhiv district), into a poor peasant family. His grandfather taught him to read when he was four, and as a boy he already worked at a sugar mill. His mother loved flowers, and he took that love from her: for his pen name he chose kosynka, a small red flower. In 1919 the paper Borotba printed his first story, At Beet Harvest, signed “Hryhorii Kosynka”.

## The book that surprised him

The first Ukrainian book Hryhorii read was The Witch of Konotop by Kvitka-Osnovianenko. It amazed him: it turned out there were people who wrote simply, the way peasants talk. In 1914 he moved to Kyiv. He took whatever work he could find, went to evening classes, passed the exams for six years of secondary school on his own, and from 1919 to 1922 studied at the Kyiv Institute of People’s Education. Later he worked for newspapers, magazines and publishers, in radio and at the Kyiv film studio.

## The village in a storm

In his lifetime Kosynka published about 20 story collections, among them Against the Golden Gods (1922), In the Wheat Fields (1926) and Politics (1927). He wrote about how the Ukrainian village lived, thought and changed during the revolution and wars of 1917–1921. Soviet critics saw him as a mere follower of [Mykhailo Kotsiubynskyi](/en/people/mykhailo-kotsiubynskyi/), Stepan Vasylchenko and Vasyl Stefanyk, but his stories are full of experiment, like those of his contemporary [Mykola Khvylovy](/en/people/mykola-khvylovyi/). In Kyiv Kosynka belonged to the literary groups Hrono, Aspys and Lanka (later MARS). In Aspys he was joined by [Mykola Zerov](/en/people/mykola-zerov/) and [Valerian Pidmohylnyi](/en/people/valerian-pidmohylnyi/), and in Lanka also by the poet [Yevhen Pluzhnyk](/en/people/yevhen-pluzhnyk/).

## The Heart that was banned

In the early 1930s Party critics accused Kosynka of “kulak ideology” and “banditry”. His stories could no longer be published, and the censors kept his collection The Heart (1933) from readers. So he wrote film scripts and translated: Chekhov, Gorky, Sholokhov and the Dead Souls of [Nikolai Gogol](/en/people/mykola-hohol/).

## December 1934

On 4 November 1934, after a critical speech at the Writers’ House, Kosynka was arrested. On 1 December the Bolshevik leader Sergei Kirov was killed in Leningrad, and the regime answered with a wave of repression. On 13–15 December a military court in Kyiv, in a fabricated case of “White Guard terrorists”, sentenced 28 people to death, among them Kosynka and the writers Dmytro Falkivskyi, Oleksa Vlyzko and Kost Burevii. Hryhorii Kosynka was shot in Kyiv on 15 December 1934. He was rehabilitated in 1957.

## Why it matters today

Since 1962 Kosynka’s books have come out in Kyiv again, and his work has been translated into Russian, Belarusian, German, Italian, Polish, Bulgarian, Hungarian and other languages. His wife, Tamara Moroz-Strilets, collected and published memoirs about him. In 1979 a monument to him was put up in his home village of Shcherbanivka.
