## One name, two meanings

Why does the word “Banderite” sound like an insult to some and a badge of pride to others? In the post-war Soviet Union it was used to brand Ukrainian nationalists, and russia still uses it that way. Yet for many Ukrainians Stepan Bandera is a symbol of resistance to both the Nazi and the Soviet regimes. To make sense of it, you have to look at the history itself.

## The road to the underground

Stepan Bandera was born in 1909 in the village of Staryi Uhryniv in Prykarpattia, the Carpathian foothills, into the family of a Greek Catholic priest. In 1927 he joined the Ukrainian Military Organization, and in 1929, as an agronomy student at the Lviv Polytechnic, the OUN. In 1933 he became head of the OUN’s regional executive in western Ukraine. In 1934, before his diploma exam, the Polish police arrested him, so he never received his agronomy degree. At the Warsaw trial of 1936 he was sentenced to death for organizing the assassination of Interior Minister Bronisław Pieracki; the sentence was commuted to life imprisonment. The same year he was tried again in Lviv.

## The 1941 Act and a Nazi camp

The OUN split in 1940, and Bandera became leader of its revolutionary wing, the OUN-B. On 30 June 1941 in Lviv his followers proclaimed the Act of Restoration of the Ukrainian State. Berlin reacted sharply, but Bandera refused to revoke the Act. He was imprisoned first in Berlin and then, from 1942 until the autumn of 1944, in the Zellenbau, a special block for political prisoners at Sachsenhausen concentration camp. His family suffered too: in 1941 a Soviet court sentenced his father, the priest Andrii Bandera, to be shot, and three of his sisters spent many years in Stalin’s camps. Two of his brothers, Oleksandr and Vasyl, died in Auschwitz in 1942.

After the war Bandera lived in exile in Munich under the name Stefan Popel and headed the OUN’s Foreign Units. On 15 October 1959 he was killed by KGB agent Bohdan Stashynskyi, with a special pistol that fired a cyanide-based poison, hidden in a newspaper.

## Debates and assessments

Bandera is one of the most controversial figures in Ukrainian history. Historians take a critical view of the OUN-B’s radical ideology, including its antisemitism and xenophobia. In 1939–1941 the OUN cooperated with German military intelligence, including in creating the Nachtigall and Roland battalions, and the text of the Act of 30 June 1941 declared cooperation with Nazi Germany. The proclamation was accompanied by pogroms in Lviv. Historians find no evidence that Bandera personally supported them, but note that he knew of the violence and did nothing to protect minorities. Historians also debate the responsibility of the movement’s leadership for the mass killings of the Polish population of Volhynia in 1943; Bandera himself was in German imprisonment at the time.

The courts ruled the 2010 decree awarding Bandera the title Hero of Ukraine unlawful (2010–2011). For decades Soviet propaganda used his name to discredit any Ukrainian independence movement.

## Why it matters today

Stepan Bandera’s story shows how complex the 20th-century struggle for independence was, caught between the Nazi and Soviet regimes. He refused to revoke the Act of Restoration of the Ukrainian State, though it cost him a Nazi concentration camp, and for many Ukrainians that became a symbol of resistance. At the same time Soviet propaganda used his name for decades, and in russia “Banderite” is still used as a brand. So his figure teaches us to tell historical facts from historians’ assessments and from propaganda. Anyone who knows this history in full is harder to sell propaganda myths about it.
