## “I am coming at you”

The Tale of Bygone Years, the old chronicle of Rus’, remembers one habit of Sviatoslav’s: before an attack he sent his enemies a short warning, “I am coming at you.” The chronicler paints him as a hero, and history remembers him as Sviatoslav the Brave.

## Olha’s son

Sviatoslav was the son of Prince Ihor and [Princess Olha](/en/people/kniahynia-olha/). The chronicle says he was born in 942; like all its early dates this one is approximate, but historians consider it likely. When his father was killed in 945, Sviatoslav was still a child, so his mother ruled Rus’ and the boy was raised by his tutor, Asmud. He began leading his own campaigns in 964.

## Marching east

In 964–966 Sviatoslav subdued the Viatichians and crushed the Khazar Khaganate, a powerful state on the Volga. He also fought the Volga Bulgars and made the Yasians and Kasogians of the North Caucasus pay tribute. That opened the way to the Caspian Sea for the merchants of Rus’.

## A capital on the Danube

The Byzantine emperor Nikephoros II Phokas asked Sviatoslav for help in a war against Bulgaria. In 967–968 the prince swept through the Danube lands; the chronicle says he took 80 towns. He liked Pereiaslavets on the Danube best of all and wanted to make it his capital. While he was away, the Pechenegs, nomads of the steppe, came up to Kyiv, and he had to return. After Olha died in 969, Sviatoslav divided Rus’ among his sons: Yaropolk in Kyiv, Oleh in the Derevlianian land and [Volodymyr](/en/people/volodymyr-velykyi/) in Novgorod.

## The siege of Dorostolon

Back in the Balkans, Sviatoslav took the Bulgarian capital, Preslav. But a new emperor, John I Tzimiskes, marched against him with a large army in 971. For about three months the Rus’ held out in Dorostolon (now Silistra in Bulgaria); then the prince made peace. His army could go home, but he gave up the Danube lands. On the way back the Pechenegs blocked his path. After wintering at Biloberezhzhia (probably near the Dnieper-Buh estuary), Sviatoslav was killed fighting the Pechenegs at the Dnieper rapids in the spring of 972. According to the chronicle, the Pecheneg khan Kuria had a cup made from his skull.

## Why it matters today

Under Sviatoslav the territory of Rus’ grew enormously, and his son Volodymyr later strengthened the state and brought Christianity to it. The warrior prince lives on in literature: Yuliian Opilskyi took his words for the title of the novel I Am Coming at You (1918), and Semen Skliarenko wrote the novel Sviatoslav (1959).
