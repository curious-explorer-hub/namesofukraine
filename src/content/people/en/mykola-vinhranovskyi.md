## A year with Dovzhenko

In 1955 a young man from the steppe town of Bohopil, in the Mykolaiv region, enrolled at the theatre institute in Kyiv to train as an actor. Then [Oleksandr Dovzhenko](/en/people/oleksandr-dovzhenko/) noticed him. The director had been allowed to take on his own class at the film institute in Moscow, and he took the young man with him. They had only a year together: Dovzhenko died in 1956. But Vinhranovskyi played the lead, the soldier Ivan Orliuk, in The Tale of Flaming Years, a film of Dovzhenko’s that Yuliia Solntseva completed. The role won him a gold medal at a film festival in Los Angeles. He later wrote a memoir of his teacher, A Year with Dovzhenko.

## The formalist who wouldn’t repent

In 1961 Vinhranovskyi came back to Kyiv famous: the film was in cinemas, and Literaturna Hazeta printed a selection of his poems. His first collection, Atomic Preludes, followed the next year. His unpublished poems circulated in samvydav (samizdat), and the poet Maksym Rylskyi, writing about the debuts of young poets including [Ivan Drach](/en/people/ivan-drach/), called Vinhranovskyi the most national of them.

When he was accused of formalism and summoned to a meeting where he was expected to repent, he asked not to be interrupted and read a poem in reply. Later Vinhranovskyi did not become a dissident like [Vasyl Stus](/en/people/vasyl-stus/), nor fall into disgrace like [Lina Kostenko](/en/people/lina-kostenko/): he stepped back from public life and went on writing and making films.

## Films, children’s books and Nalyvaiko

Vinhranovskyi directed the feature films The Shore of Hope, Duma about Brytanka and Klymko, and in the 1990s documentaries about Chyhyryn, Baturyn and Halych. He wrote poems and stories for children, among them Siromanets (1977), and in 1984 his work for children won the Shevchenko Prize. He considered the historical novel Severyn Nalyvaiko his finest work; [Ivan Dziuba](/en/people/ivan-dziuba/) wrote the preface to its first edition. From 1989 to 1993 he headed Ukrainian PEN. He died in Kyiv on 26 May 2004.

## Why it matters today

The Encyclopedia of Modern Ukraine calls Vinhranovskyi one of the brightest innovators of Ukrainian verse in the 1960s to 1980s. His poems and stories for children speak just as clearly to young readers as to adults.
