## Who he was

Ilya Repin was born in 1844 in Chuhuiv in the Kharkiv region (then Kharkiv gubernia, part of historical Sloboda Ukraine) into the family of a military settler of the Chuhuiv Uhlan Regiment. He learned to draw in Chuhuiv, in the regiment’s topographic corps and at a local icon-painting school. In 1863 he went to St Petersburg, studied at a drawing school under Ivan Kramskoi and, in 1864–1871, at the Academy of Arts. On an Academy scholarship he lived in Italy and France in 1873–1876.

## His story

Repin’s first major work was Barge Haulers on the Volga (1873). He joined the Society for Travelling Art Exhibitions (the Peredvizhniki, or Wanderers), and painted historical scenes, scenes of everyday life and portraits, including of Leo Tolstoy, Modest Mussorgsky and Nikolai Pirogov. In 1894–1907 he was a professor at the Academy of Arts and in 1898–1899 its rector. His students included the Ukrainian painters Mykola Pymonenko, Oleksandr Murashko and Fotii Krasytskyi.

Ukraine holds a prominent place in his work. Repin worked on Reply of the Zaporozhian Cossacks from 1880 to 1891, travelling around Ukraine to gather material: the Odesa and Chernihiv regions in 1880 and the Kyiv region in 1880 and 1882. The canvas shows the legend of the defiant answer that the Zaporozhian Cossacks, led by their otaman [Ivan Sirko](/en/people/ivan-sirko/), sent to the Ottoman sultan. His friend, the historian Dmytro Yavornytskyi, published a book on the Zaporozhian Cossacks illustrated by Repin. Repin also painted Evening Party, Haidamakas, Black Sea Freebooters and the unfinished Hopak, a portrait of [Taras Shevchenko](/en/people/taras-shevchenko/) (1888), illustrated Taras Bulba and Sorochyntsi Fair by [Nikolai Gogol](/en/people/mykola-hohol/), and supported drawing schools in Kyiv and Kharkiv.

From 1900 Repin lived at his Penaty estate in Kuokkala near St Petersburg. The village was part of the Grand Duchy of Finland; when Finland declared independence at the end of 1917 the village ended up outside Russia, and the painter stayed there. He last visited Chuhuiv in 1915, when he planned to open free art workshops in the town. He died in Kuokkala (today Repino, part of St Petersburg) in 1930 and, as he had wished, was buried on the estate, which has been a museum since 1940.

## Debates and assessments

Most encyclopedias and museums around the world have traditionally called Repin a Russian painter: he studied and worked mainly in St Petersburg, belonged to the Peredvizhniki, and many of his best-known canvases are devoted to Russian life and history. The Encyclopedia of the History of Ukraine calls him a Ukrainian painter; the Encyclopedia of Ukraine notes that some of his works show his attachment to Ukraine, its people and its history. What is beyond doubt is that Repin was born and grew up in Ukraine and that Ukrainian subjects were one of the important themes of his work.

After Russia’s full-scale war against Ukraine began, this debate sharpened. In early 2023 the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York changed Repin’s description in its catalog to “Ukrainian, born Russian Empire”, explaining that he was born in the Ukrainian town of Chuhuiv when it was part of the Russian Empire. The museum said it continually researches its collection to catalog it as accurately as possible. Supporters of the change, such as the journalist and historian Oksana Semenik, point to Repin’s Ukrainian landscapes and subjects. Critics, such as the historian Vartan Matiossian, consider such reclassifications of 19th-century artists hasty, replacing one oversimplification with another.

## Why it matters today

Repin’s Zaporozhian Cossacks has become one of the best-known images of the Zaporozhian Cossacks; the main canvas is in the State Russian Museum in St Petersburg, and another version is in the Kharkiv Art Museum. Chuhuiv has a museum of the painter, and monuments to him stand in Chuhuiv and Kyiv.
