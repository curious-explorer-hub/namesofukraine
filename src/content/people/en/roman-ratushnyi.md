## The Maidan at 16, and a court in Strasbourg

Roman Ratushnyi was born in 1997 in Kyiv, the son of Taras Ratushnyi, a journalist and activist in the “Save Old Kyiv” movement, and the writer Svitlana Povaliaieva. In 2012 Roman entered the Finance and Law College, where he studied law.

At 16 he joined the Revolution of Dignity and was among those hurt when the Berkut riot police attacked on the night of 30 November 2013. On 11 December he was beaten again. Roman took his case to the European Court of Human Rights, and in 2021 the court ruled that he had been ill-treated, that the investigation had not been effective and that his right to peaceful assembly had been violated.

After the Maidan Ratushnyi worked as a journalist. In 2015 he mapped the international links between the Russian and Ukrainian mafias: about a thousand people, all from public data. Today this is called OSINT.

## Tents in Protasiv Yar

Ratushnyi recorded a video appeal to Kyiv residents in 2019: a developer wanted to put up three high-rises on the slopes of Protasiv Yar in central Kyiv. He gathered activists who camped there in tents and kept watch to stop the developer’s machinery. That is how the “Protect Protasiv Yar” initiative began. When the community decided to set up an NGO, it was Roman who agreed to lead it, and the court cases became his second job. The activists were threatened, and in 2020 Roman was invited to Brussels to meet the European Endowment for Democracy and the head of the European Commission’s Support Group for Ukraine. After that the harassment stopped. In 2020 Protasiv Yar got its green-space status back, and the building permit was ruled unlawful.

## At the front

From the first days of the full-scale invasion Ratushnyi defended Kyiv, then, with the 93rd Separate Mechanized Brigade “Kholodnyi Yar”, helped liberate Trostianets and fought as a scout in the Sumy and Kharkiv regions. His call sign was “Seneca”. Roman Ratushnyi was killed near Izium in the Kharkiv region on 9 June 2022. He was 24.

## Why it matters today

Roman Ratushnyi showed that a handful of people who care can save a piece of green Kyiv if they don’t back down. “Only self-organisation will save us from the end of the world,” he said, and he lived by it, from the Maidan at 16 to defending Kyiv from the first days of the full-scale war. Even his will was for Ukraine: he left part of his military pay to the Taras Shevchenko House-Museum, and with it the museum treated a 200-year-old mulberry tree that remembers the poet. Anyone who defends their park, street or city follows in his footsteps. The Protasiv Yar reserve now bears his name, and his fellow soldiers set up the “Seneca” unit he had planned.
