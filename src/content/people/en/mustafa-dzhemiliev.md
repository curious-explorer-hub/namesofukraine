## Exiled twice

Mustafa Dzhemilev (in Crimean Tatar, Mustafa Cemilev, also known as Qırımoğlu, “son of Crimea”) was born in 1943 in the village of Ay-Serez in Crimea (today Mizhrichchia). In May 1944 the Soviet authorities deported the entire Crimean Tatar people, and the Dzhemilev family was sent to Uzbekistan. Mustafa was six months old.

Seventy years later, on 22 April 2014, the russian occupation authorities banned him from entering Crimea. For the second time, he had been driven from his homeland.

## 15 years for the right to go home

Dzhemilev fought for the Crimean Tatars’ return home from 1961 and became a co-founder of the Union of Crimean Tatar Youth. In 1965 he was expelled from his institute in Tashkent, and in 1966 he was jailed for the first time, for his part in the national movement and for refusing to serve in the Soviet Army. In 1969 he was arrested again, for criticizing the Soviet system and condemning the Warsaw Pact invasion of Czechoslovakia. In 1974 he was jailed for the third time, partly because the KGB learned he planned to hand US President Richard Nixon, then visiting the USSR, a petition from the Crimean Tatars. When a new case was opened against him in the Omsk region in 1975, the physicist Andrei Sakharov and the human rights activist Petro Hryhorenko asked the Supreme Court of the RSFSR to release Dzhemilev on their bail. He was tried several more times and spent 15 years in captivity in all. In 1986, under international pressure and after a personal appeal from US President Ronald Reagan, he was released.

## His people’s voice in Ukraine

Dzhemilev became chairman of the Mejlis of the Crimean Tatar People, its representative body, in 1991 and led it until 2013. Since 1998 he has been elected to Ukraine’s parliament again and again. That same year the UN awarded him the Nansen Medal for his contribution to resolving the statelessness and citizenship of the Crimean Tatars. In his message to the ceremony, UN Secretary-General Kofi Annan recalled that Fridtjof Nansen himself had helped Ukraine through the famine after the First World War.

In 2014–2019 Dzhemilev was the President’s Commissioner for Crimean Tatar Affairs. In 2014, as Poland marked 25 years since the elections that began the fall of communism, he became the first laureate of Poland’s new Solidarity Prize. After the occupation of Crimea he was one of the initiators of a civic economic blockade of the peninsula, and in 2022 an occupation court in Crimea gave him a two-year suspended “sentence”.

On 13 November 2023, his 80th birthday, Dzhemilev was made a Hero of Ukraine, among other things “for courage and dedication in defending the rights of the Crimean Tatar people.” Accepting the award, he said he had one more dream: to return to his homeland.

## Why it matters today

Mustafa Dzhemilev showed that a people’s rights can be defended for decades without giving an inch, even behind bars. For many Crimean Tatars and Ukrainians he is a symbol of resistance to deportation and occupation. His life reminds the world that Crimea is home to the Crimean Tatars, who have been driven out of it more than once. And his words read like advice to anyone who stands up for what is right: “freedom cannot be traded for any material goods.”
