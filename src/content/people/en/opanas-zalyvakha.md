## “And who are you?”

An art institute in Leningrad. A Czech classmate asks Panas what nationality he is, what his art is, and whether he is Russian. “No, I’m Ukrainian,” he says. And what can he tell her about Ukrainian art? Nothing. Opanas Zalyvakha was born in 1925 in the village of Husynka in the Kharkiv region, but in 1933, the years of the Holodomor, the man-made famine, his family moved to the Soviet Far East. He spent more than 30 years in Russia and had forgotten many Ukrainian words, or never known them.

## A dictation on the floor

In 1947 he was expelled from the institute for “behaviour unworthy of a Soviet student”, and only graduated in 1960. On a work placement in Kosiv, in the Hutsul highlands, Zalyvakha saw living Ukrainian village life for the first time, and in 1961 he moved to Ivano-Frankivsk.

In Kyiv he painted in [Alla Horska](/en/people/alla-horska/)’s studio, while [Ivan Svitlychnyi](/en/people/ivan-svitlychnyi/) handed him one book after another. He met [Ivan Dziuba](/en/people/ivan-dziuba/) in a hospital garden, where Dziuba sat over a pile of books by Lesia Ukrainka. Zalyvakha learned Ukrainian with a notebook for new words, and in Horska’s studio Nadiia Svitlychna read dictations to the friends. He was 35, the oldest in that “class”.

## A window that lasted one night

In 1964, for Shevchenko’s 150th anniversary, Zalyvakha, Horska, Liudmyla Semykina and other artists made a stained-glass window for Kyiv University: Shevchenko raises a book in one hand and holds up a woman with the other. On the evening of 8 March it was lit up. The next morning the artists found it covered with a sheet and broken glass on the floor: it had been destroyed on the authorities’ orders.

## “Hold on, Panas”

On 28 August 1965 officers came to search Zalyvakha’s home. Halfway through, Svitlychnyi rang the doorbell, and both were taken away. As they parted, Svitlychnyi told him: “Hold on, Panas.” In March 1966 a closed court sentenced Zalyvakha to five years in a strict-regime camp for “anti-Soviet agitation and propaganda”. In the camp in Mordovia he secretly made bookplates, greeting cards and posters of Shevchenko.

In 1970 friends, Horska among them, welcomed him back in Kyiv. In Ivano-Frankivsk he was made to work as a labourer, but kept painting.

## Why it matters today

His first exhibition in 26 years opened in Lviv in 1988. In 1995 Zalyvakha received the Shevchenko Prize, and he designed books by [Vasyl Stus](/en/people/vasyl-stus/) and [Yevhen Sverstiuk](/en/people/yevhen-sverstiuk/). At home he kept an iconostasis of friends who had died: Svitlychnyi, [Viacheslav Chornovil](/en/people/viacheslav-chornovil/), Horska. Zalyvakha died in Ivano-Frankivsk in 2007. For his centenary in 2025, artists from several Ukrainian cities gathered for an open-air painting session at places linked to his life.
