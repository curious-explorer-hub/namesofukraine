## Easy to remember

“Tsvila kalyna, tsvila Iryna”: the guelder rose blossomed, and so did Iryna. That was how Iryna Tsvila liked to introduce herself, because her surname sounds like the Ukrainian word for “blossomed”. Born in 1969 in Brovary, near Kyiv, she trained as a teacher and taught primary pupils at the Meridian international school in Kyiv. Later she became a landscape designer, planting rose gardens and greenhouses and collecting some 500 rare varieties of roses. Before the Maidan she began a website, “Chas troiand” (“Rose Time”), which she meant to be the first Ukrainian resource on roses.

## From the camera to the trenches

During the Revolution of Dignity, the 2013–2014 protests on Kyiv’s Maidan, Iryna documented events with her camera. In 2014 she volunteered for the front and for four years, until 2018, served with the Sich battalion (later the “Kyiv” special-purpose regiment). Her comrades knew her by the call sign “Linza”, “Lens”. In the battalion she was a machine gunner, a paramedic, a press officer and sometimes the cook. In Pisky she noticed that the soldiers’ first-aid kits were incomplete, so she and her husband took the “Medsanbat” course and brought back 10 NATO-standard kits and a medical backpack. She said that within a week or two those kits helped save lives. In late August 2017 a solo exhibition of her photographs opened in Sviatopetrivske, near Kyiv.

Back home in 2018, she returned to her garden and founded a workshop called Verba, making jewellery from real pressed flowers set in resin. Her daughter later joined her. They wanted a Ukrainian name, tied to their own culture and language, and found it in Lesya Ukrainka’s play The Forest Song: verba, the willow, grows back even after it is broken. Iryna treated her own surname as a motto too. She called her jewellery page “BLOOM. Tsvisty” (“to blossom”), because whatever happens, you keep living: you blossom.

## The first days of the full-scale war

On the first day of the full-scale war, 24 February 2022, Iryna and her husband joined the defence of the Kyiv region. Iryna Tsvila was killed on 25 February 2022 in the fighting for Irpin. She was 52.

## Why it matters today

Iryna Tsvila showed that you can give your country everything you know how to do: a camera, a garden and your service. Twice she stepped up to defend Ukraine, as a volunteer in 2014 and on the first day of the full-scale war, and back home she grew roses and gave her workshop a deliberately Ukrainian name. She believed everyone can bring victory closer by doing what they know how to do, not only at the front. Her example invites each of us to find our own part in that victory.
