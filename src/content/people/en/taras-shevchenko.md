## What does freedom cost?

Taras Shevchenko was born on 9 March 1814, and until he was 24 he was a serf: he and his whole family, from the village of Moryntsi in the Kyiv region (now the Cherkasy region), were a landowner’s property. He was orphaned early, but he could draw, and that talent took him to Saint Petersburg. There, artists decided to buy his freedom: the painters Ivan Soshenko and Karl Bryullov, the writer Yevhen Hrebinka and the poet Vasily Zhukovsky. Bryullov painted a portrait of Zhukovsky, the portrait was raffled off, and the 2,500 rubles it raised set Shevchenko free. That was in 1838; he went on to study at the Imperial Academy of Arts.

Shevchenko became a major artist too. He painted or drew over 150 portraits, 43 of them of himself. 835 of his artworks survive, and another 270 known works have been lost.

## The poems that scared an empire

Kobzar came out in 1840, and modern Ukrainian literature began with it. But Shevchenko also wrote poems that mocked imperial power, and he belonged to the Brotherhood of Saints Cyril and Methodius. In 1847 he was sent into exile as a soldier, to remote fortresses near the Urals and the Caspian Sea, with orders not to write or draw. The exile lasted ten years.

The ban didn’t break him. In 1848–49 Oleksii Butakov, the head of the Aral Sea expedition, got him taken on as its artist. His more than 170 drawings of the sea, its shores and the people who lived there have scientific value too: Shevchenko is believed to be the first artist to capture the Aral Sea.

After his release in 1857 he returned to Saint Petersburg and, though ill and under police watch, kept working to his last days. In 1860 the Academy of Arts, where he had once studied, made him an academician of engraving for his etchings. He died on 10 March 1861 and was first buried in Saint Petersburg, at the Smolensk Cemetery. His friends at once asked for permission to take him to Ukraine, and two months later the coffin set off home. In Kyiv, students unhitched the horses and pulled the coffin across the Chain Bridge themselves, and the steamer Kremenchuk carried it on to Kaniv. Shevchenko was buried above the Dnipro, on Chernecha Hill, just as he had asked in “Testament”.

## Not a gloomy old man

We are used to seeing Shevchenko as a stern older man in a fur hat and coat. But as a young man in Saint Petersburg he earned well from his portraits and dressed in fashion. He was short, 164 cm, and loved to sing: when he was in a good mood among friends, he would sing his favourite Ukrainian songs. His favourite drink was tea, to which he often added rum, and his favourite dish was borscht with dried crucian carp.

## Why it matters today

Shevchenko proved that great literature could be written in Ukrainian: modern Ukrainian literature began with his Kobzar. He put into words the ideas Ukrainian national identity grew from: freedom, dignity and resistance to oppression. His life shows that words are stronger than bondage: neither serfdom nor exile with orders not to write could silence him. He also wanted people to be able to read Ukrainian: the last book he published in his lifetime was a primer for Sunday schools, in 1861. It had 10,000 copies at 3 kopecks each, the cheapest textbook of its day, and Shevchenko paid for it himself. He raised the money the way his own freedom had once been bought: he raffled off his self-portrait. According to Ukraine’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs, there are 1,384 monuments to Shevchenko in the world: 1,256 in Ukraine and 128 in 35 other countries. And his call “Fight on, and you will win!” still rings out today for everyone defending their freedom.
