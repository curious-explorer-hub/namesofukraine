## “A distorted image of Shevchenko”

Did you know that an artist could be thrown out of the Union of Artists because of Shevchenko? In 1964 Liudmyla Semykina was expelled “for an ideologically flawed work that gives a distorted image of T. Shevchenko”. The work was a stained-glass window for the poet’s 150th anniversary.

## Odesa, the sea, Kyiv

Semykina was born in 1924 in Odesa. She studied at the art school there, then graduated from the Kyiv Art Institute in 1953. Her early paintings are of the Odesa port, its piers and the sea. From 1962 she began making clothes based on Ukrainian folk dress, and in 1963 she joined the Club of Creative Youth, where the Sixtiers gathered.

## A window for the university

In 1964 Semykina, [Opanas Zalyvakha](/en/people/opanas-zalyvakha/), [Alla Horska](/en/people/alla-horska/), [Halyna Sevruk](/en/people/halyna-sevruk/) and Halyna Zubchenko made the stained-glass window Shevchenko. Mother for the foyer of Kyiv University. That same year it was destroyed on the authorities’ orders, and Semykina was expelled from the Union. She was reinstated in 1966.

## Twenty years outside

In 1968 she signed the “letter of 139” against unlawful repression and closed trials in Ukraine. She was expelled again and only let back into the Union in 1988, “for lack of any fact of accusation”. She never stopped working: in 1970–1971 she was chief costume designer of the film Zakhar Berkut. In the 1990s she created whole series of garments: Princely Age, Polissia Legend, Scythian Steppe, High Castle. For them she took the shapes of medieval Ukrainian clothing and restrained, refined ornament. In 1997 she received the Shevchenko Prize.

She wrote a memoir about her friend Alla Horska, The Victor of Her Time. Liudmyla Semykina died in Kyiv in 2021, at 96.

## Why it matters today

Liudmyla Semykina showed that old Ukrainian dress is more than a museum piece: from princely, Polissian and Scythian motifs she made clothes for today. For twenty years the authorities kept her out of the Union, and she went on doing her work. Anyone who looks to Ukrainian tradition today to make something new is following in her footsteps.
