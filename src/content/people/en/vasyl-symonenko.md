## An obituary for a corncob

Can you write an obituary for a corncob? Vasyl Symonenko did. It was a satire on the Soviet system, and it spread through samvydav (samizdat): texts that people retyped and passed from hand to hand, out of the censors’ reach.

## A journalist in Cherkasy

Symonenko was born in 1935 in the village of Biivtsi in the Poltava region into a peasant family and lost his father early. He began writing poetry at Kyiv University, where he studied journalism and led a student literary studio. After graduating he worked for newspapers in Cherkasy and was the Cherkasy correspondent of Robitnycha Hazeta.

Because of censorship, Symonenko published little in his lifetime. His only collection, Silence and Thunder, came out in 1962, and that same year he was admitted to the Writers’ Union of Ukraine. His best-known poems, though, were the samvydav ones: satires, poems about the hard life of the peasants, about resisting despotism and great-power chauvinism, and a cycle of poems about his love for Ukraine.

## Secret graves near Kyiv

Where did the NKVD bury the people it shot in Kyiv? In 1962 Symonenko, together with [Alla Horska](/en/people/alla-horska/) and [Les Taniuk](/en/people/les-taniuk/), found those places: at Bykivnia and in the Lukianivka and Vasylkiv cemeteries. They demanded that the authorities make the burials public and turn them into places of remembrance, and the KGB put Symonenko on its watch list. That same summer, police at the railway station in Smila, in the Cherkasy region, detained him and beat him brutally.

Symonenko died in Cherkasy on 13 December 1963, at the age of 28, more than a year after the beating. The official cause was kidney cancer; according to an unofficial version, the beating hastened the illness. The collections Earth’s Gravity and Wine from Roses and the children’s fairy tale A Journey to the Land of Backwards came out after his death, and in the diaspora The Shore of Waiting. Since 1964 the Ukrainian publishing house Smoloskyp, based abroad, has borne Symonenko’s name. At first Soviet critics suppressed his samvydav poems while praising him as a poet devoted to the Party line; later his works were suppressed entirely. During the arrests of 1972, as [Ivan Svitlychnyi](/en/people/ivan-svitlychnyi/) recalled, the authorities opened a case even against the dead poet.

## Why it matters today

Vasyl Symonenko lived only 28 years and published a single collection, yet censorship couldn’t stop his words: people retyped his poems themselves and passed them from hand to hand. In 1965 he was nominated for the Shevchenko Prize, but he received it only 30 years later, posthumously, in independent Ukraine. His satire and his poems of love for Ukraine became one of the sources of the Sixtiers’ resistance and helped many feel that they didn’t have to stay silent. He showed that one person with a pencil can shake a system that controls every printed word. His lines “You know that you are a human being. / Do you know it or not?” still speak to every reader.
