## Did Picasso really bow before her?

Mariia Prymachenko’s work was shown at the 1937 World’s Fair in Paris. Ever since, people have quoted Pablo Picasso as saying, on seeing it, that he bowed before “the artistic miracle of this brilliant Ukrainian woman”. It’s a lovely story, but no one cites a primary source for the line.

What we do know: a year before Paris, her work won first prize at an exhibition of folk art in Kyiv, Moscow and Leningrad. Not bad for a self-taught artist.

## The girl from Bolotnia

Mariia Prymachenko was born in 1909 in the village of Bolotnia in the Kyiv region, near Ivankiv, and lived there almost all her life. Her father was a master carpenter, her mother an embroiderer, and her grandmother painted pysanky, Easter eggs. As a child Mariia had polio and was left disabled. She taught herself to paint: once she gathered blue clay in the hem of her skirt and painted the family house with it. The Kyiv embroiderer Tetiana Floru saw her work, and in 1935–1938 Prymachenko worked in Kyiv, at experimental workshops in the Kyiv Pechersk Lavra.

## Beasts that don’t exist

She painted flowers in unusual colors, birds as signs of goodness and peace, and fantastic “beasts” that became her trademark. She began with watercolor, moved on to gouache, and from the mid-1960s swapped white backgrounds for colored ones. She illustrated children’s books, made a series about the Chornobyl disaster in 1986, and created more than 800 works in all. In 1966 she won the Shevchenko Republican Prize for a series of decorative panels, “For the Joy of People”, and for her 100th anniversary UNESCO declared 2009 the Year of Mariia Prymachenko. The minor planet 14624 Prymachenko is named after her. Her son Fedir became an artist too.

Her beasts inspired others, sometimes too literally: in 2013 a designer at the Finnish company Marimekko admitted that she had copied her “Metsänväki” (“Forest Dwellers”) print from a 1963 Prymachenko painting, and apologized.

## Why it matters today

Mariia Prymachenko, a self-taught artist from a village in Polissia, created a world of her own that is honored far beyond Ukraine. She showed that world-class art can grow out of folk tradition and one’s own land. When her paintings were carried out of the fire in Ivankiv in 2022, her beasts and doves became one of the symbols of Ukraine’s resilience. Her caption on a poppy painting reads as a wish to us all: “I give red poppies to people, so they would love the holy land and work on it.”
