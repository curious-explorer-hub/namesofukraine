## The pamphlet that opened the West’s eyes

Why did people the war had thrown into Germany refuse to go home? In 1945, in Augsburg, Ivan Bahrianyi wrote a pamphlet about it: “Why I Don’t Want to Go Back to the USSR”. He spoke up for fellow countrymen who were being forcibly sent back to the Soviet Union. The pamphlet was translated into English, Spanish, Italian and other languages, and it changed how the Western world treated refugees from the USSR.

## A poet they couldn’t break

Ivan Lozoviaha was born in 1906 in Okhtyrka, in today’s Sumy region. He studied at the Kyiv Art Institute and joined MARS, a circle of writers who stood in opposition to the regime. With [Valerian Pidmohylnyi](/en/people/valerian-pidmohylnyi/) and other friends he travelled around Ukraine by bicycle to see how people really lived. Censors cut his poems, and his poem “Ave Maria”, which he published at his own expense, was confiscated.

In 1932 he was arrested. Under interrogation he said: “I am a Ukrainian writer.” He was then exiled to the Soviet Far East. In 1938 he was arrested again and spent almost two years under interrogation in a Kharkiv prison. He was released “for lack of evidence”.

## Tigers, taiga and the Garden of Gethsemane

What he lived through became novels. The Tiger Hunters is an adventure story about Ukrainians exiled to the Far East; its three English editions alone sold over a million copies. The Garden of Gethsemane tells of the torture chambers the author went through himself. It was one of the first books to tell the truth about the Gulag, written two decades before Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn’s The Gulag Archipelago.

## Ukraine’s voice in exile

During the Second World War Bahrianyi helped the Ukrainian underground. In 1944 he emigrated, and from 1946 he lived in Neu-Ulm in Bavaria. There he and like-minded people founded a party with its own newspaper, Ukrainian News; he led the Ukrainian National Council and was vice-president of the Ukrainian People’s Republic in exile. He died in Germany on 25 August 1963.

## Why it matters today

At home his books were banned for decades. In 1992 Ivan Bahrianyi was posthumously awarded the Shevchenko Prize. His example is a reminder that the truth about unfreedom can be told even when telling it is punished.
