## The choir that told the world about Ukraine

Did you know that one of the loudest voices of the young Ukrainian People’s Republic abroad was a choir? In 1919 Oleksandr Koshyts’s Ukrainian Republic Capella set off on tour to tell the world about Ukrainians and their fight for independence. It sang in 17 countries in Europe and the Americas, and “Shchedryk” travelled the world with it.

## A priest’s son from Shevchenko country

Koshyts was born in 1875 in the village of Romashky near Kyiv, the son of a priest. He grew up in Tarasivka, not far from Kyrylivka, where [Taras Shevchenko](/en/people/taras-shevchenko/) spent his childhood. As a seminary student he was already writing down folk songs there. At the Kyiv Theological Academy he led the choir and brought back the music of Artem Vedel, which the church authorities had long banned.

After the academy Koshyts taught in Stavropol and set up a city choir there whose repertoire was three-quarters Ukrainian. When the choir marked Mykola Lysenko’s jubilee without permission, Koshyts had to leave his job. Over three summer expeditions in 1903-1905 he recorded about a thousand songs in the Kuban, most of them historical and military. The collection won him a gold medal at an exhibition, but the notebooks were later lost: the fate of only two is known.

## A student choir and “Shchedryk”

In Kyiv Koshyts studied at [Mykola Lysenko](/en/people/mykola-lysenko/)’s music school and led the Kyiv University student choir. From 1912 to 1916 he was chorus master at [Mykola Sadovskyi](/en/people/mykola-sadovskyi/)’s theatre. [Mykola Leontovych](/en/people/mykola-leontovych/)’s “Shchedryk” was first heard at a concert as early as 1913, though imperial censors kept the word “Ukrainian” off the posters. The famous performance came on 29 December 1916, by Koshyts’s student choir.

## Seventeen countries in a few years

In 1919, on the orders of [Symon Petliura](/en/people/symon-petliura/), Koshyts and the composer Kyrylo Stetsenko set up the Ukrainian Republic Capella. Its first big success came in Prague on 11 May 1919. Austria, Switzerland, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Britain, Germany, Poland and Spain followed.

The capella was later renamed the Ukrainian National Chorus, and in 1922 it crossed the Atlantic. On 5 October 1922 “Shchedryk” was sung at Carnegie Hall in New York. The choir went on to Canada, Mexico, Cuba, Brazil, Argentina and Uruguay.

## The road that didn’t lead home

While the choir was abroad, the Ukrainian People’s Republic fell, and Koshyts could not go back with it. In the late 1920s he asked the Soviet authorities for permission to return to Ukraine and was refused. He settled in New York, led combined Ukrainian choirs and wrote church music, including five liturgies. From 1941 he spent his summers teaching choir conductors in Winnipeg, Canada, where he died on 21 September 1944.

## Why it matters today

Koshyts showed that a song can do for a country what diplomats can’t always manage: make the world learn about it. Thanks to his choir, Ukrainian music was heard in 17 countries, and “Shchedryk” became part of Christmas around the world. The capella’s tour is considered the first project of Ukrainian cultural diplomacy. Anyone who takes Ukrainian culture to the world today is following the road Koshyts opened.
