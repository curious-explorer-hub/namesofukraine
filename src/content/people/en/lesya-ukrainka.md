## What does a nine-year-old write about?

Larysa Kosach wrote her first poem, “Hope”, when she was nine. It was prompted by news of her aunt, who had been exiled for taking part in the revolutionary movement. The girl grew up in an educated family in Novohrad-Volynskyi (now Zviahel): her mother was the writer Olena Pchilka, and her uncle was the scholar and public figure [Mykhailo Drahomanov](/en/people/mykhailo-drahomanov/). Larysa learned to read at four. At 13 she published a poem for the first time, “Lily of the Valley”, in the Lviv magazine Zoria. That was also when the pen name “Lesya Ukrainka” appeared, much like her uncle’s signature: Drahomanov sometimes signed himself “Ukrainets” (“a Ukrainian”).

## Stronger than her illness

As a child Larysa fell ill with tuberculosis, and she spent her whole life seeking treatment: in Crimea, Egypt, Italy and Georgia. Her illness also made her give up a career as a pianist. But all that time she wrote. She wrote the poem The Possessed in a single night in 1901, at the bedside of her dying friend Serhii Merzhynskyi. She knew more than a dozen languages, and at 19 she wrote a textbook for her younger sisters, Ancient History of the Eastern Peoples. She wrote about what stirred all of Europe: freedom, dignity, the choice between submission and struggle. Her poem “Contra spem spero!” (“Without hope, I hope!”) became a symbol of resilience for many. She wrote The Forest Song, inspired by the legends of Volhynia, in 10–12 days in Kutaisi, Georgia, and it is one of the best-known Ukrainian plays.

In 1908 she paid out of her own pocket for an expedition by the folklorist Filaret Kolessa that recorded Ukrainian dumy on a phonograph. Her husband was the musicologist Klyment Kvitka. Lesya Ukrainka died in the small Georgian town of Surami at 42 and is buried at Baikove Cemetery in Kyiv.

## Why it matters today

Lesya Ukrainka proved that strength of spirit can be greater than illness: she spent her whole life seeking treatment, and all that time she wrote. Writing in Ukrainian about what stirred all of Europe, freedom, dignity, the choice between submission and struggle, she brought Ukrainian literature into that European conversation. Her “Contra spem spero!” (“Without hope, I hope!”) still speaks to anyone who refuses to give up: you can live and create even when it is hard. As early as 1898 Ivan Franko wrote that this “sick, frail girl” was “almost the only man in all of modern, united Ukraine”. The asteroid 2616 Lesya bears her name, and the 200-hryvnia banknote carries her call: “For truth, brothers, let us unite sincerely.”
