## Hundreds of thousands of donations a month

More than 450,000 separate contributions and about 300 million hryvnias (around 7 million dollars) a month: that, according to the foundation itself, is how the Sternenko Community Foundation works (as of September 2025). Most of the money comes in small sums from ordinary Ukrainians. The foundation connects makers of FPV drones, which a pilot flies through a camera, with army units, pays for the drones and delivers them to the front. It says it has given the military more than 210,000 drones since the full-scale war began.

## An activist from Odesa

Serhii Sternenko was born in 1995 in the village of Sadove in Odesa Oblast. In January 2014 he joined the Euromaidan protests in Odesa, and in March he became head of the Odesa branch of Right Sector, a nationalist group, which he left in 2017. He helped organize pickets of the Russian consulate and the disruption of concerts by the singers Ani Lorak and Svitlana Loboda in Odesa, and studied law at Odesa’s Mechnikov University. In 2018 he was attacked three times in Odesa. His YouTube channel, where he talks about events in Ukraine, has millions of subscribers.

## From territorial defence to the defence ministry

On 24 February 2022, the day of the full-scale invasion, Sternenko joined Kyiv’s territorial defence, and later focused on volunteering. The foundation he set up became one of Ukraine’s biggest crowdfunders for drones, and on 4 December 2025 the president gave Sternenko the Golden Heart award for his volunteer work. On 22 January 2026 Defence Minister Mykhailo Fedorov made Sternenko his adviser on the use of drones at the front.

## Debates and assessments

In 2015 a case was opened against Sternenko over the alleged kidnapping of a district councillor, Serhii Shcherbych. In February 2021 a district court in Odesa found him guilty of kidnapping, robbery and illegal possession of a weapon and sentenced him to 7 years and 3 months in prison. In May 2021 the appeal court reduced the sentence: it kept the kidnapping and weapons counts and gave him 3 years, suspended with 1 year of probation. On 10 February 2022 the Supreme Court overturned the ruling on the weapons count (keeping a single cartridge) and upheld the kidnapping verdict. Sternenko denies wrongdoing, calls the case politically motivated and said he would take it to the European Court of Human Rights.

A separate case concerned the third attack on him, in May 2018: one of the attackers, Ivan Kuznetsov, died of a stab wound. In 2020 the SBU named Sternenko a suspect in intentional homicide; he insisted he had been defending his life. The Ukrainian Helsinki Human Rights Union called the prosecution politically motivated. A number of politicians publicly accused him of murder, among them members of Opposition Platform – For Life and Servant of the People, while those who spoke up for him included former health minister Ulana Suprun and MPs from the Holos party. On 26 December 2023 Sternenko said a district court in Odesa had closed the case; at that point the court had not published the decision.

## Why it matters

Sternenko’s story shows how civic activism and an online audience can become part of a country’s defence: small donations from hundreds of thousands of people every month turn into drones for the front.
