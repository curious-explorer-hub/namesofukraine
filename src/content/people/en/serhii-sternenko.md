## Hundreds of thousands of donations a month

More than 450,000 separate contributions and about 300 million hryvnias (around 7 million dollars) a month: that, according to the foundation itself, is how the Sternenko Community Foundation works (as of September 2025). Most of the money comes in small sums from ordinary Ukrainians. The foundation connects makers of FPV drones, which a pilot flies through a camera, with army units, pays for the drones and delivers them to the front. It says it has given the military more than 210,000 drones since the full-scale war began.

## An activist from Odesa

Serhii Sternenko was born in 1995 in the village of Sadove in Odesa Oblast. In January 2014 he joined the Euromaidan protests in Odesa, and in March he became head of the Odesa branch of Right Sector, a nationalist group, which he left in 2017. He helped organize pickets of the Russian consulate and the disruption of concerts by the singers Ani Lorak and Svitlana Loboda in Odesa, and studied law at Odesa’s Mechnikov University. In 2018 he was attacked three times in Odesa. His YouTube channel, where he talks about events in Ukraine, has millions of subscribers. The Palianytsia Awards named him Person of the Year in Ukrainian YouTube.

## From volunteering to the defence ministry

After the full-scale invasion began in 2022, Sternenko joined Honor, a unit that is part of the Special Operations Forces, and later focused on volunteering. The foundation he set up became one of Ukraine’s biggest crowdfunders for drones. On 1 May 2025 he was attacked for the fourth time: a woman with a pistol wounded him and was detained on the spot. The SBU said Russian special services had recruited her remotely. On the night of 30 November 2025 it was Sternenko who reported that Ukrainian Sting interceptor drones had, for the first time, shot down several jet-powered Shaheds. In December 2025 the president gave him the Golden Heart award. On 22 January 2026 Defence Minister Mykhailo Fedorov made Sternenko his adviser on the use of drones at the front. In July 2026, after Fedorov’s dismissal, Sternenko stopped being the minister’s adviser and said his foundation would keep supplying the military, though on a smaller scale.

## Debates and assessments

In 2015 a case was opened against Sternenko over the alleged kidnapping of a district councillor, Serhii Shcherbych. In February 2021 a district court in Odesa found him guilty of kidnapping, robbery and illegal possession of a weapon and sentenced him to 7 years and 3 months in prison. In May 2021 the appeal court acquitted him of robbery and let the kidnapping finding stand, but punished him only on the weapons count: 3 years, replaced with a 1-year suspended sentence. On 10 February 2022 the Supreme Court closed the weapons part (keeping a single cartridge) as too minor an offence, and upheld the kidnapping finding but released Sternenko from liability because the time limit had run out. Sternenko denies any part in the kidnapping and calls the case fabricated.

A separate case concerned the third attack on him, in May 2018: one of the attackers, Ivan Kuznetsov, died of a stab wound. In 2020 the SBU named Sternenko a suspect in intentional homicide; he insisted he had been defending his life. The Ukrainian Helsinki Human Rights Union called the prosecution politically motivated. In 2020 those ready to stand surety for him included former health minister Ulana Suprun and MPs from the Holos party. On 26 December 2023 Sternenko said a district court in Odesa had closed the case; at that point the court had not published the decision.

## Why it matters today

Sternenko’s story shows how civic activism and an online audience can become part of a country’s defence. His foundation runs mostly on small sums from ordinary Ukrainians, proof that defending the country is the work not only of the army and the state but of everyone who can help. A small donation, made by hundreds of thousands of people, becomes real help for the front.
