## A boy from Romanivka

What do a medieval Kyivan fresco, a village icon and the wall of a Soviet theatre have in common? For Mykhailo Boichuk, everything: these were the roots from which he wanted new Ukrainian art to grow. He was born in 1882 in the village of Romanivka near Terebovlia, in Galicia. He learned to paint in Lviv, at the studio of Yuliian Pankevych, and then at the art academies of Vienna, Kraków and Munich. His studies were paid for by the Shevchenko Scientific Society and Metropolitan [Andrei Sheptytskyi](/en/people/andrei-sheptytskyi/).

## Byzantium in Paris

In Paris in 1909 Boichuk opened his own studio of neo-Byzantine art, and his school grew out of it. That year he showed his work at the Salon d’Automne, and in 1910 he and his students exhibited at the Salon des Indépendants. His ideas caught the attention of French critics, among them the poet Guillaume Apollinaire.

## Back home

Back in Lviv, Boichuk rescued old 15th- and 16th-century icons at the National Museum. He painted the icon The Protection of the Mother of God, which includes a portrait of Metropolitan Sheptytskyi, as well as portraits of [Mykhailo Hrushevskyi](/en/people/mykhailo-hrushevskyi/). In 1912–1914 he also worked in the Chernihiv region, painting a church in the village of Lemeshi. In 1914, at the start of the First World War, the Russian authorities interned him as an Austro-Hungarian subject.

## A school that painted walls

In 1917 Boichuk became one of the founders of the Ukrainian Academy of Arts in Kyiv, where he taught monumental art. A school formed around him, the Boichukists, among them Ivan Padalka, Vasyl Sedliar and Oksana Pavlenko. Together they painted murals for the Lutsk Barracks in Kyiv (1919), a peasant sanatorium on the Khadzhybei Estuary in Odesa (1928) and the Chervonozavodskyi Theatre in Kharkiv (1933–1935).

## Arrest and execution

On 25 November 1936 Boichuk was arrested. He was accused of “Ukrainian bourgeois nationalism” and of leading a made-up terrorist organization. On 13 July 1937 he was shot in Kyiv, on the same day as his students Ivan Padalka and Vasyl Sedliar. His wife, the artist [Sofiia Nalepynska-Boichuk](/en/people/sofiia-nalepynska-boichuk/), was also shot later. Most of Boichuk’s works were destroyed: in 1952, 14 of his paintings were removed from the National Museum in Lviv and destroyed as “ideologically harmful”.

## Why it matters today

Mykhailo Boichuk showed that new art can grow from its own roots: medieval Kyivan frescoes, icons and folk painting. These ideas caught the attention of critics in Paris, among them Guillaume Apollinaire, and his search ran close to that of the Mexican muralist Diego Rivera. At home he rescued old icons, helped found the Ukrainian Academy of Arts and raised a whole school, the Boichukists, who painted walls with him in Kyiv, Odesa and Kharkiv. Most of that work was destroyed, but what survived is being seen again. And his idea still holds: you don’t have to give up what is yours to make something modern.
