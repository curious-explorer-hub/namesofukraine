## The lawyer who built a People’s House

What did the future “dictator” do before politics? He was a small-town lawyer. Yevhen Petrushevych was born in 1863 in the town of Busk near Lviv, the son of a Greek Catholic priest, and studied law at Lviv University. In 1897 he opened a law office in Sokal, where he headed the local Prosvita society, helped found a savings bank and built a People’s House for the town.

## From Sokal to the imperial parliament

In 1907 Petrushevych was elected to the Austrian parliament in Vienna, and in 1917 he became head of the Ukrainian Parliamentary Representation. By the autumn of 1918 Austria-Hungary was falling apart. On 18–19 October a Ukrainian Constituent Assembly met in Lviv and elected the Ukrainian National Rada, with Petrushevych as its president. The Rada proclaimed a Ukrainian state on the empire’s Ukrainian lands. And when [Dmytro Vitovskyi](/en/people/dmytro-vitovskyi/)’s soldiers took Lviv on the night of 1 November, Petrushevych was in talks in Vienna.

## Union and dictatorship

Poland claimed Galicia too, and the Ukrainian-Polish war began. On 3 January 1919 in Stanyslaviv, Petrushevych opened the Rada’s first session, which passed a law on union with Ukraine east of the Zbruch. On 22 January the Act of Union was proclaimed in Kyiv, and he joined the Directory of the Ukrainian People’s Republic. By spring the front had rolled east, and Lviv stayed in Polish hands. On 9 June, in Zalishchyky, all civil and military power was handed to Petrushevych, and he was proclaimed Plenipotentiary Dictator. Right then the Galician Army was attacking from Chortkiv and pushed the Poles back almost to Lviv, but the Entente allowed Poland to occupy all of Galicia. In July the army and government crossed the Zbruch to Kamianets-Podilskyi.

## Diplomacy without an army

With no army and no land left, Petrushevych fought with words. In November 1919 he left through Romania for Vienna and set up a Western Ukrainian government in exile there. He travelled to Paris, London, Geneva and Genoa seeking recognition for an independent Galicia, and turned down Polish offers of autonomy in return for accepting Polish rule. In March 1923 the Entente’s Conference of Ambassadors gave Eastern Galicia to Poland, and Petrushevych dissolved his government. He spent the rest of his life in Berlin, in poverty; among those who helped him was the former hetman [Pavlo Skoropadskyi](/en/people/pavlo-skoropadskyi/). In 1938, after [Yevhen Konovalets](/en/people/yevhen-konovalets/) was assassinated, Petrushevych handed his authority as commander of the Western Ukrainian armed forces to Konovalets’s successor, Andrii Melnyk. He died in Berlin in 1940, and on 1 November 2002 he was reburied at Lychakiv Cemetery in Lviv.

## Debates and assessments

No law of the Western Ukrainian republic provided for a dictatorship, so researchers consider its legal basis shaky; they also note that he did not abuse his power and consulted those around him. The Directory refused to recognise the dictatorship and set up its own ministry for Galicia. Petrushevych clashed sharply with [Symon Petliura](/en/people/symon-petliura/): Petliura sought an alliance with Poland, while Petrushevych saw Poland as the greatest enemy and pinned more hope on the White russian forces backed by the Entente. In the autumn of 1919 the Galician Army made a deal with Denikin’s Whites. In the 1920s Petrushevych hoped the Soviets would help free Galicia from Poland, but he gave up that hope after the Holodomor and the end of Ukrainisation. Contemporaries and historians call him honest and uncompromising, but agree that he often misjudged foreign policy.

## Why it matters today

Yevhen Petrushevych led the first state of Galicia’s Ukrainians and brought it into union with Ukraine to the east, when Ukrainians from east and west first joined in one state. His path from the Prosvita society in Sokal to parliament shows how everyday civic work prepares a people for a state of its own. Even after losing his army and his land, he spent years telling the world that Galicia had a right to freedom, and he refused to trade it for autonomy under Polish rule. Anyone who stands up for Ukraine in the world’s meeting rooms today carries on his work.
