## The Declaration of Independence, in exile

The Kazakh steppe, the late 1970s. An exile from Kyiv, just out of a labour camp, is translating Thomas Jefferson’s Declaration of Independence into Ukrainian, along with poems by Robert Burns and Edgar Allan Poe and prose by Somerset Maugham. This was how Valerii Marchenko served his sentence for what he had written and said.

## Articles nobody was meant to read

Valerii Marchenko was born in Kyiv in 1947. He studied philology at Kyiv University, did an internship in Baku and started out translating from Azerbaijani and Polish. From 1970 he worked at the newspaper Literaturna Ukraina.

The KGB asked him to work for them, and he refused. After that he was watched, and the essays he had written for samvydav, the uncensored writing passed from hand to hand, were declared anti-Soviet. On 25 June 1973 Marchenko was arrested, among other things for reading and lending out [Ivan Dziuba](/en/people/ivan-dziuba/)’s book Internationalism or Russification? The sentence: 6 years in strict-regime camps and 2 years of exile. He knew that with his kidney disease a long camp term could cost him his life.

## A chronicle from behind the barbed wire

In the camps Marchenko gathered information about prisoners’ conditions and secretly sent it out, together with statements and essays. In 1981 he came back to Kyiv, worked as a watchman and went on writing protests against the abuses of the authorities.

On 21 October 1983 he was arrested again, and on the same day he joined the Ukrainian Helsinki Group. Although he was gravely ill, the court gave him 10 years in special-regime camps and 5 years of exile, and he was sent to a camp in the Perm region. The medical department of the Soviet Interior Ministry concluded that he should be released as incurably ill, but the KGB would not allow it. Valerii Marchenko died on 7 October 1984 in a prison hospital in Leningrad, aged 37. His mother obtained his body and buried her son in the village of Hatne near Kyiv.

## Among the Sixtiers

The Internet Encyclopedia of Ukraine names Marchenko among the Sixtiers who were given long sentences, alongside [Ivan Svitlychnyi](/en/people/ivan-svitlychnyi/), [Vasyl Stus](/en/people/vasyl-stus/) and [Yevhen Sverstiuk](/en/people/yevhen-sverstiuk/). Marchenko and Stus both died in captivity. Marchenko was rehabilitated in 1991. Today a school in Kyiv and a street in Bucha bear his name, and a monument to him stands in Hatne.
