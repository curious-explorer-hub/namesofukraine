## The poet who brought back Rome

Mykola Zerov was born in 1890 in Zinkiv in the Poltava region. His father, a school inspector, would not tolerate Ukrainian at home, while his mother spoke Ukrainian. As a student Zerov became a conscious Ukrainian: in 1910 he spoke on behalf of the students at Borys Hrinchenko’s grave. In 1914 he graduated in history and philology from Kyiv University, then taught history and Latin at gymnasiums and edited the bibliographic journal Knyhar. In the famine year of 1920 he moved to Baryshivka near Kyiv, where until 1923 he taught, translated a great deal and wrote his own poems. He then became a professor in Kyiv and later headed departments of the history of Ukrainian literature and of translation theory.

Zerov became the intellectual leader of the Kyiv Neoclassicists, poets who in the 1920s championed classical European values, craftsmanship and taste. He wrote polished sonnets, and his Anthology of Roman Poetry (1920) and the collection Kamena (1924) opened the Roman poets to Ukrainian readers. In 1925 his Ukrainian version of Juliusz Słowacki’s drama Mazepa came out. As a critic Zerov wrote about [Ivan Franko](/en/people/ivan-franko/), [Lesya Ukrainka](/en/people/lesya-ukrainka/), Panteleimon Kulish, Pavlo Tychyna and Maksym Rylskyi.

## “To the sources!”

Zerov met [Mykola Khvylovy](/en/people/mykola-khvylovyi/) in December 1923, and they soon became allies against “proletarian hackwork”. During the Literary Discussion of 1925–1928, at a debate on 24 May 1925, Zerov backed Khvylovy’s call to learn from cultured Europe and added the slogan “Ad fontes!”. Later this alliance was held against both men as a grave political crime.

## Solovki and Sandarmokh

The pressure began in 1934: Zerov was dismissed from the university and left for Moscow. In April 1935 he was arrested near Moscow and sent to Kyiv for investigation. At an interrogation the investigators read Panteleimon Kulish’s poem “To the Kobza”, which Zerov had recited at Rylskyi’s flat, as praise of “political fighters of the past”. Zerov argued back: the poem was about the power of the word. Under pressure from NKVD interrogators he confessed to belonging to a mythical “counter-revolutionary nationalist organization”, and in 1936 he was given 10 years in the camps. He served his term on the Solovetsky Islands. His health kept him from logging, so he cleaned the camp buildings and in his free time translated Virgil’s Aeneid. On 19 September 1937 he wrote to his wife that he had finished it. The manuscript has never been found. On 9 October 1937 a special NKVD troika sentenced him to death. Zerov and [Les Kurbas](/en/people/les-kurbas/) were among the Ukrainian prisoners shot at that time in the Sandarmokh forest in Karelia; Zerov was shot on 3 November 1937. His wife was not told and went on sending him money for eight more months. In 1957 a Soviet death record gave a false date, 13 October 1941. He was rehabilitated in 1958.

## Why it matters today

Zerov and the Neoclassicists proved that Ukrainian poetry could be part of the great European tradition. His translations opened the Roman poets to Ukrainian readers, and his slogan “Ad fontes!” (“To the sources!”) called for learning from cultured Europe rather than settling for “proletarian hackwork”. He showed that Ukrainian deserves the same craft as Latin. In 2020 the Mykola Ostrovsky Park in Kyiv’s Solomianskyi district was renamed Mykola Zerov Park. Anyone today who translates world classics into Ukrainian, or writes with care for form, follows his path.
