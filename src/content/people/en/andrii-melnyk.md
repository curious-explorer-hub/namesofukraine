## Coming home after 60 years

Why were two coffins carried from Luxembourg to Kyiv in May 2026? They held the remains of Andrii Melnyk, one of the leaders of the Ukrainian nationalist movement, and his wife Sofia. For more than 60 years they had lain in a cemetery in Luxembourg. On 25 May 2026 they were reburied with state honours at the National Military Memorial Cemetery near Kyiv. Some welcomed the decision; others criticized it sharply.

## The forester who became a rifleman

Andrii Melnyk was born in 1890 in the village of Volia Yakubova near Drohobych and studied forestry in Vienna. When the First World War broke out, he volunteered for the Legion of Ukrainian Sich Riflemen and commanded a company in the battles of Makivka and Lysonia. In 1916 he was wounded and taken prisoner by the russians. In a camp near Tsaritsyn he met [Yevhen Konovalets](/en/people/yevhen-konovalets/) and became one of his closest allies.

Around the turn of 1918 Melnyk escaped to Kyiv and, together with Konovalets, built up the Sich Riflemen, holding command posts. In 1919 he was chief of staff of the UNR army. He later held the rank of otaman, a general, but called himself a colonel.

## From the UVO to the head of the OUN

In 1920 Melnyk was one of the founders of the underground Ukrainian Military Organization (UVO), and from 1922 he led it in Galicia. The Polish authorities arrested him and sentenced him to five years in prison. Released in 1928, he worked as a forester on the estates of Metropolitan [Andrei Sheptytskyi](/en/people/andrei-sheptytskyi/) and led the Catholic youth organization Orly ("Eagles").

In 1938 a Soviet agent killed Konovalets, and Melnyk took over the Leadership of Ukrainian Nationalists. In 1939 the OUN's Second Grand Assembly in Rome confirmed him as leader. But in early 1940 younger radicals led by [Stepan Bandera](/en/people/stepan-bandera/) refused to accept his leadership, and the OUN split. Melnyk led its more moderate wing, which became known as the OUN-M.

## Watched by the Gestapo

In 1941, after Germany attacked the USSR, Melnyk's expeditionary groups set out east for Kyiv. There, in the autumn, they set up a Ukrainian National Council, but the Nazi authorities broke it up and shot many OUN members, among other places at Babyn Yar. Among those killed was the poet [Olena Teliha](/en/people/olena-teliha/), a member of Melnyk's wing. Melnyk himself lived in Berlin under German house arrest from 1941. In 1944 the Gestapo arrested him, and he spent several months in the Sachsenhausen concentration camp. After his release he refused to head the German-sponsored Ukrainian National Committee.

## A voice for the emigration

After the war Melnyk settled in Luxembourg. In 1947 he was elected the OUN's leader for life. In 1957 he proposed calling a world congress of Ukrainians. The World Congress of Free Ukrainians was founded in 1967, after his death. Melnyk died on 1 November 1964.

## Debates and assessments

From the start of his leadership Melnyk counted on German support and had contacts with German military intelligence (the Abwehr). Scholars note that he kept to this course even under house arrest. At the same time, in 1942 he and other Ukrainian leaders, Metropolitan Sheptytskyi among them, sent Hitler a memorandum demanding an end to Germany's destructive policy in Ukraine. In 1943 some OUN-M units in Volhynia joined attacks on Polish villages on their own initiative. Some Polish historians hold that Melnyk bears no responsibility for this: the decisions were taken by local commanders, and the OUN-M leadership condemned such actions in 1944.

The 2026 reburial was controversial. Israel's Foreign Ministry and the Yad Vashem Holocaust memorial criticized the state honours for the leader of a movement that collaborated with Nazi Germany. Critics point out that the OUN spread antisemitic rhetoric in those years and that some of its members took part in the persecution of Jews.

## Why it matters today

Melnyk linked three stages of the fight for independence: the Sich Riflemen and the UNR army, the underground between the wars, and the Ukrainian emigration after the Second World War. His path shows how narrow the space was for Ukrainians caught between two totalitarian regimes: he counted on Germany and ended up under its arrest and in a concentration camp. In exile he worked to get Ukrainian communities scattered around the world to act together, and his idea of a world congress came true. His story teaches us to see history whole: the contribution, the mistakes and the debates that still go on.
