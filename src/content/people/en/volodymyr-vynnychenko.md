## Who he was

Volodymyr Vynnychenko was born in 1880 into a peasant family in the village of Veselyi Kut near Yelysavethrad (today part of Kropyvnytskyi in central Ukraine). He studied at the Yelysavethrad gymnasium, entered the law faculty of Kyiv University, but in 1902 he was expelled for political activity. He joined the Revolutionary Ukrainian Party and later became one of the leaders of the Ukrainian Social Democratic Workers’ Party. He was jailed in Kyiv several times, and in 1907–1914 he lived in exile in Austria, Switzerland and France.

His literary debut, the short story “Beauty and Strength” (1902), was welcomed by [Ivan Franko](/en/people/ivan-franko/) and [Lesya Ukrainka](/en/people/lesya-ukrainka/). In the 1910s he became a popular writer, and his plays were staged by Ukrainian, Russian and other European theaters. During the First World War Vynnychenko lived illegally in Moscow and worked with the journal Ukrainskaia zhizn, edited by [Symon Petliura](/en/people/symon-petliura/).

## His story

After the February Revolution of 1917 Vynnychenko returned to Kyiv and, together with [Mykhailo Hrushevskyi](/en/people/mykhailo-hrushevskyi/), led the Ukrainian national movement: he became deputy head of the Central Rada (Ukraine’s revolutionary parliament) and then head of the General Secretariat, the first Ukrainian government. He led the delegation that presented Ukraine’s demand for autonomy to the Russian Provisional Government, and he wrote or co-wrote all four Universals (manifestos) of the Central Rada. For a time Vynnychenko favored a federal union with Russia, but in January 1918, on the eve of the Bolshevik advance on Kyiv, he backed the independence proclaimed by the Fourth Universal. That same month he resigned as head of the government.

Under Hetman [Pavlo Skoropadskyi](/en/people/pavlo-skoropadskyi/) Vynnychenko was in opposition: in September 1918 he became head of the Ukrainian National Union, and on 14 November head of the Directory of the Ukrainian People’s Republic (UPR), which rose against the hetman and took power. On 22 January 1919 the union of the UPR and the West Ukrainian People’s Republic was proclaimed in Kyiv. Already on 11 February 1919 Vynnychenko left the Directory: the Entente powers, whose support the government was counting on, did not want a socialist at the head of the UPR, and he himself disagreed with that course. Petliura took over as head of the Directory.

In exile Vynnychenko declared that he had become a communist, set up the Foreign Group of the Ukrainian Communist Party in Vienna and wrote his three-volume memoir Rebirth of a Nation. In 1920 he spent several months in Moscow negotiating with the Bolsheviks. He was offered the posts of deputy head of government and people’s commissar for foreign affairs of Soviet Ukraine, but was refused a seat on the Politburo of the Ukrainian Communist Party, and he left again. From 1925 he lived in France, and from the mid-1930s on an estate in Mougins near Cannes, where he wrote novels, developed his philosophy of “concordism” (harmony of a person with oneself, society and nature) and painted. In 1933 he sent an open letter to the Politburo protesting against the Holodomor, the man-made famine, after which his works were banned in the USSR. In 1941 he refused to collaborate with the German occupation authorities and spent several weeks in a concentration camp. He died in Mougins in 1951.

## Debates and assessments

Vynnychenko’s political views are judged in different ways. The Encyclopedia of the History of Ukraine notes that he considered himself a Marxist but was strongly influenced by Western liberalism, and that he wavered between the idea of full independence and that of a Ukrainian Soviet republic. Critics point to his federalist views of 1917 and his talks with the Bolsheviks in 1920, when, in the words of the Encyclopedia of Modern Ukraine, he wanted to “dress Bolshevism in Ukrainian clothes”. His defenders stress that his key demand was an independent, united Ukraine, that he turned down posts without real influence, and that he later sharply criticized Bolshevik centralism and publicly protested against the Holodomor.

His conflicts with fellow leaders were sharp too. He left the Directory because he disagreed with its more right-wing, Entente-oriented course, and his relations with Petliura are a research topic of their own (for example, Valerii Soldatenko’s book Vynnychenko and Petliura, 2007). In emigration harsher accusations were heard: the very title of Petro Solukha’s book (New York, 1973), Treaty with Moscow against Hetman Pavlo Skoropadskyi, accuses Vynnychenko and Mykyta Shapoval of going “to Moscow, to Lenin, for help”.

His fiction also divided readers. Contemporaries criticized the idea of “honesty with oneself”, which Vynnychenko developed in the novel of that name, as extreme individualism and amorality. In the USSR his works were banned from the 1930s until the late 1980s.

## Why it matters today

Vynnychenko headed Ukraine’s first government and co-wrote the Universals of the Central Rada, including the Fourth Universal, which proclaimed the independence of the UPR. His fiction and plays returned to Ukrainian readers in the late 1980s; his archive is kept at Columbia University in New York, and in 2021 an English collection of his plays, Disharmony and Other Plays, was published.
