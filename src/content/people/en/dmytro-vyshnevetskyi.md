## Seven fortresses on one island

On the Dnipro near Khortytsia lies Baida Island, or Mala Khortytsia, just 570 meters long. Archaeologists have found seven layers of settlements and fortifications here, one beneath another, from the most ancient times to the 18th century. One of those layers holds the remains of a small town with a fortress built in the mid-16th century by Prince Dmytro Vyshnevetskyi.

## A prince on a restless frontier

Vyshnevetskyi came from a Volhynian princely family; the year of his birth is not known for certain. Through his mother the family was related to the Ostrozkyi princes. From the 1540s Dmytro served as starosta (royal district governor) of Cherkasy and Kaniv, on the southern frontier of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, where Tatar raids had to be fought off constantly. He relied on the Zaporozhian Cossacks.

In the 1550s Vyshnevetskyi built a powerful fortification on Mala Khortytsia, and in 1554 King Sigismund II Augustus appointed him guard of Khortytsia. From there he led campaigns against the Turkish fortress of Ochakiv and the Tatar Islam-Kermen near today’s Kakhovka. In autumn 1556 his Cossacks took Islam-Kermen, carried off its cannons and brought them to his castle.

The angry Crimean khan first tried to win the prince over, sending him gifts and a letter inviting him into his service. Vyshnevetskyi refused. In winter 1557 the khan besieged the fortress for 24 days, but failed to take it and pulled back with heavy losses, and the king thanked Vyshnevetskyi for his "steadfastness and brave defence". Later that year the khan came back with an even larger army. When the defenders ran out of food, Vyshnevetskyi had to abandon the island.

## Between tsar, king and sultan

When the help the Lithuanian and Polish authorities had promised never came, Vyshnevetskyi entered the service of Muscovite Tsar Ivan IV and fought against the Crimean Khanate. He later returned to the allegiance of Sigismund II.

In 1563 Vyshnevetskyi set out for Moldavia: some Moldavian boyars who did not want Tomșa as their ruler had invited him to take the throne. During the campaign he was wounded and captured, then taken to Istanbul. On 15 October 1563 the French ambassador in Istanbul wrote that "poor Dimitrashko", as the prince was called there, would arrive that day or the next morning. Historians write that Vyshnevetskyi refused to enter Turkish service. On 29 October 1563, on the sultan's orders, he was executed together with the Polish nobleman Piasecki.

## Was he Baida?

Legends about his death began to spread in Ukraine, Poland and Lithuania right after it happened. According to tradition, it inspired the folk duma about the Cossack Baida, who refuses to serve the sultan. Whether Vyshnevetskyi really was the model for Baida is debated by historians.

## Why it matters today

Dmytro Vyshnevetskyi is called the first Cossack otaman in the history of Ukraine, and the Khortytsia reserve calls his town and fortress on Mala Khortytsia the first Zaporozhian Sich in history. His Moldavian campaign is believed to have paved the way for a whole line of Cossack leaders, up to Tymish Khmelnytskyi. The folk duma about the Cossack Baida keeps the image of a man who will not serve the sultan, even in captivity. Anyone who hears of Khortytsia and the Sich today is hearing about a beginning he made.
