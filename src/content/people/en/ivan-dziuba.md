## Who he was

Ivan Dziuba was born in 1931 in the village of Mykolaivka in the Donetsk region. He graduated from the Donetsk Pedagogical Institute (1953) and did postgraduate studies at the Institute of Literature in Kyiv. He published his first criticism in 1950, and in 1959 brought out the book “An Ordinary Person” or a Philistine?, which attacked formulaic, whitewashed Soviet literature. He became one of the spokesmen of the Sixtiers (shistdesiatnyky), the generation that emerged during Khrushchev’s “Thaw”, among them [Lina Kostenko](/en/people/lina-kostenko/) and [Vasyl Symonenko](/en/people/vasyl-symonenko/). His speeches at evenings in memory of Shevchenko, Lesya Ukrainka and Symonenko drew wide attention. He was dismissed from the journal Vitchyzna for “ideological errors”.

## His story

On 4 September 1965, at the Kyiv premiere of [Sergei Parajanov](/en/people/serhii-paradzhanov/)’s film Shadows of Forgotten Ancestors at the Ukraina cinema, Dziuba addressed the audience and announced the secret arrests of young Ukrainian intellectuals. [Viacheslav Chornovil](/en/people/viacheslav-chornovil/) and [Vasyl Stus](/en/people/vasyl-stus/) called on the audience to stand up in protest. It was one of the first public protests against political repression in the USSR. On 29 September 1966, at Babyn Yar, Dziuba spoke out against official and popular antisemitism.

At the end of 1965 he sent the leaders of Soviet Ukraine his essay Internationalism or Russification?. In it Dziuba showed how the authorities were Russifying Ukraine under the banner of “internationalism”, and called for a return to Lenin’s nationality policy, which in his view had been distorted by great-power chauvinism. The essay spread through samvydav, was published in the West in many languages and became the manifesto of the Ukrainian Sixtiers. In 1972 Dziuba was arrested and expelled from the Writers’ Union, and in 1973 he was sentenced to 5 years’ imprisonment. Later that year, after a public recantation, he was pardoned and released; he was rehabilitated in 1991.

In the late 1980s Dziuba returned to public life: he was one of the founders of the People’s Movement of Ukraine (Rukh), minister of culture in 1992–1994 and, from 1992, a member of the National Academy of Sciences. He chaired the Shevchenko National Prize Committee and co-chaired the editorial board of the Encyclopedia of Modern Ukraine (for which he wrote, among others, the entry on [Mykola Zerov](/en/people/mykola-zerov/)). He wrote a book on [Mykola Khvylovy](/en/people/mykola-khvylovyi/) and the monograph Taras Shevchenko: Life and Work on [Taras Shevchenko](/en/people/taras-shevchenko/). He died in Kyiv on 22 February 2022, two days before Russia’s full-scale invasion.

## Debates and assessments

The most controversial episode of Dziuba’s life is his recantation of 1973. After his conviction he wrote a public renunciation of his views and was released in November 1973. Some dissidents, notably Valentyn Moroz and Leonid Pliushch, condemned him sharply for it. Later came his book Facets of a Crystal on the literatures of the peoples of the USSR, which the Encyclopedia of Ukraine regards as a repudiation of Internationalism or Russification?; the Encyclopedia of Modern Ukraine, by contrast, says that in these studies he worked “ostensibly to defend the ideas of internationalism” while trying to show each nation’s undistorted face. Even so, in independent Ukraine Dziuba again became one of the most respected intellectuals: an academician, a minister and a Hero of Ukraine.

## Why it matters today

Internationalism or Russification? remains one of the most important texts on the Russification of Ukraine. Dziuba showed that one person armed with words can challenge a system, and later how culture can be rebuilt in an independent state.
