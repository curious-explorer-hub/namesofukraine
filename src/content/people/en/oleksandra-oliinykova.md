## A Croatian flag, a Ukrainian passport

Oleksandra Oliynykova was born in 2001 in Kyiv. When she was ten, her family left for Croatia: her father, Denys Oliynykov, had come under pressure for protesting against Viktor Yanukovych’s government. There she took up tennis seriously and, to make her way in the sport easier, became a Croatian citizen. In 2018 she won her first ITF title under the Croatian flag. In 2021, before the full-scale invasion, she decided to take back her Ukrainian citizenship.

## The year it all came together

Her father, who had coached her since childhood, went back to Ukraine in 2024 and joined the armed forces. She started travelling to tournaments on her own. In 2025 Oliynykova won three WTA 125 titles and broke into the world top 100. In January 2026, at the Australian Open, she played her first ever main-draw match at WTA level, against the defending champion, Madison Keys. She led 4–0 in the first set but lost 6–7, 1–6. Her father watched from Ukraine.

## Ukraine’s voice on court

Oliynykova lives and trains in Kyiv. Before the Australian Open she described her flat during the blackouts: no electricity, no water, no heat. She came to her press conference in Melbourne in a T-shirt that read: “I need your help to protect Ukrainian children and women, but I can’t talk about it here.” Since then, at every tournament, she has reminded reporters and fans of the war Russia started against Ukraine, and she doesn’t shake hands with Russian or Belarusian opponents.

At Roland-Garros in 2026 she reached the third round, where she lost to Russia’s Diana Shnaider, and afterwards spoke about the war again: she won’t stay silent, because her future is in Ukraine. That year she climbed to No. 44 in the world.

## Money for her father’s unit

Oliynykova uses her fame to raise money for the unit her father serves in. In the summer of 2026 she planned a charity auction for it in Toronto, but the WTA cancelled it under its rules on player credentials.

## Why it matters today

Oliynykova could have stayed a Croatian player, but in 2021, before the full-scale invasion, she chose Ukraine. She kept training in Kyiv even when her flat had no electricity or heat, won three WTA 125 titles in 2025 and climbed to No. 44 in the world in 2026. She uses every big tournament to keep the world from getting used to the war: “Some players are tired to speak about this now. I’m not.” And she turns her fame into help, raising money for the unit her father fights in and co-founding the drones4ua.org drive for drones. She shows that an athlete can be her country’s voice.
