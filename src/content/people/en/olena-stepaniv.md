## The girl the guards recognized

While still a history and geography student at Lviv University, Olena Stepaniv led the women's platoon of the Sich Riflemen II society: 33 women among 300 riflemen. In 1914, when the Ukrainian Sich Riflemen Legion was being formed in Lviv, she showed up to enlist disguised as a man. The photographer taking her picture for a rifleman's ID recognized her as a girl and called the gendarmes. A lawyer, Volodymyr Starosolskyi, talked the command into signing her up for the Legion anyway.

## An officer for the battle of Makivka

The Legion fought the battle for Mount Makivka in the Carpathians in May 1915. Stepaniv fought in it. She was decorated for bravery in the Carpathian battles and promoted to khorunzha - a junior officer rank. In late May 1915, near Bolekhiv, she was captured and sent to Tashkent, where she spent the next two years as a prisoner of war.

In April 1917 she travelled home from captivity through Finland and Sweden - and became a celebrity there: Finnish women had their photos taken with her at every station, and in Sweden she met Sven Hedin, the explorer of Central Asia. Later Stepaniv joined the Legion's Supreme Military Board, which organized the November uprising of Ukrainians in Lviv against Austro-Hungarian rule. Through 1918 and 1919 she commanded a platoon in the Ukrainian Galician Army.

## A geographer after the war

After the war Stepaniv earned a doctorate in history and geography from the University of Vienna (1921) and returned to Lviv to teach geography at the Basilian Sisters' gymnasium. Polish authorities banned her from teaching in 1935 over her Ukrainian civic activity, so she went on to head the guidance department of the Ridna Shkola society instead. She wrote more than 75 scholarly works, including a memoir of the war (1930), a study of contemporary Lviv (1943) and, the same year, an essay, "Crimea (the key to the Black Sea)", arguing that Crimea naturally belongs to Ukraine.

Surprisingly, the famous "woman soldier" did not urge women to join the army en masse: in a 1939 interview with the writer Iryna Vilde she firmly opposed it, and saw her own decision to go to war as something sacred.

## Ten years in a labour camp

The Soviet authorities arrested Stepaniv in 1949 and in 1950 sentenced her to ten years in labour camps in Mordovia. She returned to Lviv only in 1956 and died there in 1963; she is buried at Lychakiv Cemetery.

## Why it matters today

Olena Stepaniv proved you can fight for Ukraine even when they won’t let you into the ranks: she became one of very few women officers in any army of the First World War. After the war she built Ukrainian scholarship and schooling just as stubbornly, and when she was banned from teaching, she found another way to serve education. Neither a Polish ban nor a Soviet camp made her give it up. Since 2015 the mayor of Lviv has presented an annual Olena Stepaniv Award to women with outstanding achievements in education, science, culture and public life. Her story is a reminder that the “firsts” of Ukrainian history often earned that word not through one feat, but through an entire life.
