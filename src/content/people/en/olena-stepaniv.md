## The girl the guards recognized

In 1914, when the Ukrainian Sich Riflemen Legion was being formed in Lviv, Olena Stepaniv - a history and geography student at Lviv University - showed up to enlist disguised as a man. The guards spotted her and wanted her detained. A lawyer, Volodymyr Starosolskyi, talked the recruiters into signing her up anyway. Still a student, she was soon given a platoon to command.

## An officer for the battle of Makivka

In May 1915 the Legion fought the battle for Mount Makivka in the Carpathians. Stepaniv fought in it, was decorated for bravery, and promoted to khorunzha - a junior officer rank. Weeks later, on 30 May 1915, she was captured and sent to Tashkent, where she spent the next two years as a prisoner of war.

Back from captivity in 1918, Stepaniv joined the Legion's Supreme Military Board, which organized the November uprising of Ukrainians in Lviv against Austro-Hungarian rule. Through 1918 and 1919 she commanded a platoon in the Ukrainian Galician Army.

## A geographer after the war

After the war Stepaniv earned a doctorate in history and geography from the University of Vienna (1921) and returned to Lviv to teach geography at the Basilian Sisters' gymnasium. Polish authorities banned her from teaching in 1935 over her Ukrainian civic activity, so she went on to head the guidance department of the Ridna Shkola society instead. She wrote more than 75 scholarly works, including a memoir of the war (1930) and a study of contemporary Lviv (1943).

## Ten years in a labour camp

In 1949 the Soviet authorities sentenced Stepaniv to ten years for "Ukrainian nationalism" and sent her to a labour camp in Mordovia. Her property was confiscated and her documents destroyed. She returned to Lviv only in 1956 and died there in 1963; she is buried at Lychakiv Cemetery.

## Why she still matters

Olena Stepaniv's life ran through war, scholarship and repression, one after another: she fought for a Ukrainian state, helped build Ukrainian geography as a science, and paid for it with ten years of unfreedom. Her story is a reminder that the "firsts" of Ukrainian history often earned that word not through one feat, but through an entire life.
