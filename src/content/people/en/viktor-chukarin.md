## His mother didn't know him

In 1945 a young man came home from German captivity so worn down that at first his mother didn't recognise him. She knew him by a scar on his head. Seven years later that young man, Viktor Chukarin, was the best gymnast at the Olympic Games.

## From the Azov coast to Kyiv

Chukarin was born in 1921 in the village of Krasnoarmiiske on the Azov coast. When he was three, his family moved to Mariupol. There he studied at a metallurgical college, then transferred to the Kyiv College of Physical Culture. In 1940 he won the Ukrainian gymnastics championship in Kharkiv.

## Four years as a prisoner

When the Second World War broke out, Chukarin served in the artillery. In July 1941 he was wounded near Poltava and taken prisoner. He spent almost four years in German concentration camps, Sandbostel and Buchenwald among them. When he was freed, he weighed 40 kilograms.

## Lviv and two Olympics

In 1946 Chukarin came to Lviv and enrolled at the Institute of Physical Culture. He rebuilt his strength in the gym and competed for Lviv sports clubs. In 1952 the Soviet team went to the Olympics for the first time, and in Helsinki the 30-year-old Chukarin became all-around champion. He won four gold medals, in the all-around, the team event, the pommel horse and the vault, plus two silvers. In the women's competition the all-around title went to another Ukrainian, Mariia Horokhovska.

In 1954 Chukarin became world all-around champion, and in 1956 in Melbourne he won the Olympic all-around a second time. In all he won 11 Olympic medals, a record at the time.

## Teacher of Lviv's gymnasts

After competing, Chukarin stayed in Lviv and taught at the Institute of Physical Culture, and in 1971 he became head of its gymnastics department. He trained more than twenty masters of sport, among them the 1980 Olympic champion Bohdan Makuts. Chukarin died in Lviv in 1984 and is buried in Lychakiv Cemetery.

## Why it matters today

Chukarin came through captivity and the camps, then rebuilt himself through his own hard work and became the best gymnast in the world. He won his medals under the Soviet flag, but his path ran through Mariupol, Kyiv and Lviv, and it was in Lviv that he stayed to teach the next generations of champions. His life shows that war can take years from a person but not their will. Anyone who has to start again from nothing can remember the young man his mother didn't recognise, who seven years later stood first at the Olympics.
