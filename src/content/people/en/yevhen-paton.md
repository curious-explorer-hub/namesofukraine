## The boy from Nice

Yevhen Paton was born in 1870 in Nice, where his father was the Russian consul. He graduated from the polytechnic in Dresden (1894), but the Russian Empire didn’t recognize foreign degrees. So Paton took the exams at the St Petersburg Institute of Railway Engineers and earned a second diploma in 1896.

## Bridges for Kyiv

In 1904 Paton was invited to the Kyiv Polytechnic Institute, where he headed the department of bridges. Over his career he produced about 35 bridge designs and wrote textbooks on bridge building. After the revolution he rebuilt destroyed bridges, among them a bridge across the Dnipro in Kyiv that opened in 1925.

## Starting over at almost 60

In 1929 Paton was elected to the Academy of Sciences. That same year, at almost 60, he took up something new. Bridges were then held together by rivets, and Paton believed the future lay in welding, which was still poorly understood. He set up an electric welding laboratory at the Academy, and in 1934 turned it into the Institute of Electric Welding, which he led for the rest of his life. His team developed high-speed automatic submerged-arc welding, ten times more productive than welding by hand.

## Welding for the war

In the Second World War the institute was evacuated to Nizhny Tagil in the Urals. There Paton and his staff set up automatic welding of tank armour and a production line for tank hulls. In 1943 he became the first Ukrainian academician to be named a Hero of Socialist Labour. From 1945 to 1952 Paton was vice-president of the Academy of Sciences of the Ukrainian SSR.

## The bridge he never saw

Paton’s last great project was a bridge across the Dnipro in Kyiv, about one and a half kilometres long: Europe’s largest all-welded road bridge. Yevhen Paton died on 12 August 1953, less than three months before it opened, and the bridge was named after him.

## Why it matters today

The Institute of Electric Welding bears Yevhen Paton’s name. His school was carried on by his son, [Borys Paton](/en/people/borys-paton/), who took over the institute in 1953 after his father’s death and later led the whole Academy of Sciences of Ukraine.
