## A Cossack family

The Miklukho-Maklai family is traced to a Zaporozhian Cossack captain, Stepan Makukha. Later the family was made noble and changed its name to the smoother-sounding Miklukha. Mykola's father, a railway engineer, came from Starodub county in the Chernihiv region. He admired [Taras Shevchenko](/en/people/taras-shevchenko/), petitioned for the poet's release from exile and helped him with money, and in 1856 he lost his post for it. Mykola was born in 1846; sources differ on where: the village of Rozhdestvenskoye near Borovichi (now in Russia) or Malyn in the Zhytomyr region.

## The student who was thrown out

In 1864 Mykola was expelled from St Petersburg University for taking part in student unrest. He carried on in Germany, at Heidelberg, Leipzig and Jena, where he studied medicine. Trips to the Canary Islands (1866) and Italy (1867) drew him into anthropology and ethnography. He also studied sea creatures off Odesa and the southern coast of Crimea.

## A hut on Astrolabe Bay

How does a scientist win the trust of a people he has never met? In September 1871 Miklukho-Maklai landed on the north-east coast of New Guinea, by Astrolabe Bay, and built himself a hut. He won over the local people with patience, courage and a doctor's skills. In all he spent about three years among the Papuans. He argued that all human races are one and equal, and that there are no "lower races". He also fought the slave trade: on his advice the Dutch authorities moved in 1878 to stop the traffic in people on the islands of Ternate and Tidore.

## Sydney and Malyn

In Australia, Miklukho-Maklai pressed for a marine biological station in Sydney, and it was built at Watsons Bay. In 1884 he married Margaret Clark there. In 1873 his mother had bought an estate near Malyn, then in the Kyiv province (now the Zhytomyr region), and he visited it in 1886 and 1887. Miklukho-Maklai died in St Petersburg in 1888, aged 41.

## Why it matters today

The north-east coast of New Guinea is still called the Maclay Coast. His diary, Among the Savages of New Guinea, was translated into Ukrainian by [Borys Antonenko-Davydovych](/en/people/borys-antonenko-davydovych/). And the idea he defended among the Papuans, that no people is higher or lower than another, still sounds modern.
