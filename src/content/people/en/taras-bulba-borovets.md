## Where did the first UPA come from?

Most people link the name “Ukrainian Insurgent Army” (UPA) with Roman Shukhevych and the OUN(b). But another force took the name first, as early as December 1941. It was led by an otaman from Polissia, Taras Borovets, known by the nom de guerre “Bulba”. Later the OUN(b) built a UPA of its own, and a bitter struggle began between the two armies.

## A boy from the quarry

Taras Borovets was born on 9 March 1908 in the village of Bystrychi, in the Rivne region, into a poor peasant family. The family often lived on potatoes alone, which is probably how the boy got the nickname “Bulba”, a Ukrainian word for potato. At first it annoyed him, but at school he read [Nikolai Gogol](/en/people/mykola-hohol/)’s novel Taras Bulba, and its hero became his idol. Taras was working in a stone quarry by the age of 14 and later ran a granite quarry of his own. Volhynia was then part of Poland. In 1934 the Polish authorities arrested him on suspicion of links to the OUN and for an anti-Polish book he had written, Mr Deputy in the Sejm, and sentenced him to three years. He ended up in the Bereza Kartuska prison camp, but was released early, in 1935.

## The Olevsk Republic

In 1939–1941, while the USSR held Volhynia, Borovets built an underground network in Polissia on the instructions of [Andrii Livytskyi](/en/people/andrii-livytskyi/), head of the Ukrainian People’s Republic in exile. After Germany attacked the USSR, it grew into a force called the Polissian Sich, which fought the remnants of Soviet units. On 21 August 1941 his men took the town of Olevsk in the Zhytomyr region, and for almost three months the so-called Olevsk Republic existed there. Schools reopened, sometimes with Soviet textbooks but with lessons meant to raise Ukrainian patriots, and the Sich had its own print shop and a newspaper, Haidamaka. Officially the Germans treated the Sich only as a Ukrainian militia and refused to recognise it as a military unit, so on 15 November 1941 Borovets disbanded it. He went underground, and from then on his units operated under the name UPA. By 1942 they were fighting the German occupiers, including a raid on the important railway junction at Shepetivka, and Borovets himself demanded that Erich Koch, the Reich Commissar for Ukraine, stop the requisitions and arrests.

## Between two armies

In 1943 the OUN(b) demanded that Borovets place his units under its political leadership. He refused: his force recognised the authority of the Ukrainian People’s Republic government in exile. To set his army apart from the OUN(b)’s UPA, in July 1943 he renamed it the Ukrainian People’s Revolutionary Army (UNRA). On 10 August Borovets wrote an open letter to the OUN(b) leadership, accusing it of one-party dictatorship and the ethnic cleansing of Poles, and calling for an end to the infighting. On the night after 18 August, OUN(b) units surrounded his headquarters near the village of Khmelivka. Many of his fighters were captured, among them his wife Anna; researchers believe she was later killed.

## Talks and a concentration camp

In late 1943 Borovets went to negotiate with the German authorities, but he was arrested in Berlin. He was taken to the Sachsenhausen concentration camp, where [Stepan Bandera](/en/people/stepan-bandera/) was also being held. Borovets was released in 1944.

## Life without a state

After the war Borovets lived in West Germany, where he founded the Ukrainian National Guard and published the journal Mech i volia (Sword and Freedom). He later moved to the United States and withdrew from active politics. His memoir, Army Without a State, came out in Winnipeg in 1981. Taras Bulba-Borovets died in New York on 15 May 1981, at the age of 73.

## Debates and assessments

Some scholars call Borovets the founder of the UPA, since his force was the first to take the name. Others stress that the army that went down in history as the UPA was the OUN(b)’s, and that his own memoir contains a good many myths. His contacts with the German authorities are judged in different ways too: in 1941 he sought the occupiers’ recognition of the Sich, and in 1943 he negotiated with them. His supporters point out that he never won any recognition, fought the occupiers from 1942 and ended up in a concentration camp.

The sharpest question is the fate of the Jews of Olevsk. In November 1941, after the Sich had been disbanded, a German detachment shot hundreds of the town’s Jews. According to testimony and documents gathered by Holocaust researchers, former Sich fighters took part in the shooting, and members of the Sich had carried out pogroms against Olevsk’s Jews as early as the summer and autumn of 1941. Other historians cite documents showing that the Sich council refused to take part in the shootings, and that Borovets warned a rabbi of the danger and condemned the killing. According to the same documents, he opposed reprisals against civilians of any nationality. Polish historians write that, according to recent research, his fighters, apart from those forced into OUN(b) units, had nothing to do with the mass killings of Poles in Volhynia: they clashed several times with the Polish Home Army but did not carry out mass killings of civilians. At the same time, they note, Poles at the time often did not tell the two UPAs apart. His supporters see the armed conflict with the OUN(b) in 1943 as a fratricidal blow to the insurgent movement, while the OUN(b) sought to bring all insurgent units under its own leadership. How to judge Bulba-Borovets remains a matter of debate.

## Why it matters today

Taras Bulba-Borovets was among the first to take up arms in Polissia in 1941 for a Ukrainian state, caught between two totalitarian empires. He kept his oath to the Ukrainian People’s Republic even when it meant fighting other Ukrainians. His story shows how high the price of division was: an army without a state and without unity lost its strength, and many people died. Knowing the whole story, its dark pages included, helps explain why unity and the truth about the past still matter so much to Ukraine today.
