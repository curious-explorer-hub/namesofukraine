## The president who taught children to read

Did you know that the first and only president of Carpatho-Ukraine had spent more than twenty years running a college for teachers and writing primers for schoolchildren?

He was born in 1874 in Kelechyn, a mountain village in Zakarpattia, into a priest’s family. At the time the region belonged to Hungary. Voloshyn studied in Uzhhorod and Budapest, where he qualified as a teacher of mathematics and physics. In 1897 he became a Greek Catholic priest.

## Primers, newspapers and a language

From 1900 Voloshyn taught at the Uzhhorod Teachers’ Seminary, and from 1917 to 1938 he was its director. He wrote more than 40 books: a primer, readers, grammars and textbooks on teaching, as well as fiction under the pen name Andrii Verkhovynskyi. In 1907 he published a grammar in Hungarian arguing that the speech of the people of Zakarpattia was part of the Ukrainian language. When the Hungarian authorities wanted church and school books in Hungarian and the Latin alphabet, Voloshyn stood up firmly for Cyrillic. He edited the newspapers Nauka and Svoboda, and in 1920 he was one of the founders of the Prosvita society in Uzhhorod.

After the First World War Zakarpattia became part of Czechoslovakia. Voloshyn led the Christian People’s Party and from 1925 to 1929 sat in the Czechoslovak parliament. In 1933 he was made a papal prelate, with the title of Monsignor. The Voloshyns had no children of their own, so they supported an orphanage in Uzhhorod and gave it their house.

## A state for a few days

In autumn 1938 Czechoslovakia granted the region autonomy, and on 26 October Voloshyn became head of its government, which later worked from Khust. On 14 March 1939 Voloshyn proclaimed the independence of Carpatho-Ukraine. The next day the Diet in Khust confirmed it: Ukrainian became the state language, blue and yellow the colours of the flag, and “Shche ne vmerla Ukraina”, with words by [Pavlo Chubynskyi](/en/people/pavlo-chubynskyi/) and music by [Mykhailo Verbytskyi](/en/people/mykhailo-verbytskyi/), the anthem. The deputies elected Voloshyn president unanimously.

By then the Hungarian army was already advancing. On 16 March the Carpathian Sich fought their biggest battle at Krasne Pole near Khust. That same day Voloshyn left with part of the government through Romania, and on 18 March the last defenders stopped resisting.

## Prague and Moscow

In Prague Voloshyn taught pedagogy at the Ukrainian Free University, and in 1945 he became its rector. In May 1945 the Soviet secret services arrested him and took him to Moscow. Avhustyn Voloshyn died there in prison on 19 July 1945, aged 71. The official cause given was cardiac paralysis. Where he was buried is not known.

In 2002 Voloshyn was posthumously made a Hero of Ukraine, and his order and award papers went to the Zakarpattia Regional Museum to be kept there for good.

## Debates and assessments

Communist propaganda called Voloshyn a collaborator: his government sought Germany’s support, and he himself asked Hitler to recognize Carpatho-Ukraine as a state. These efforts failed, and Germany let Hungary occupy the region. Other writers note that the governments in Prague and Bratislava looked to Germany in the same way at the time, because Czechoslovakia had no other way out. At the same time, Voloshyn’s diplomacy is considered to have achieved little: his state was left isolated.

## Why it matters today

Avhustyn Voloshyn showed that the Ukrainians of Zakarpattia are part of one people, and he proved it not with slogans but with primers, newspapers and a school for teachers. When the moment of choice came, he proclaimed an independent Ukrainian state with the same flag, anthem and language that Ukraine has today. Carpatho-Ukraine lasted only a few days, but it showed the world that Ukrainians were ready to defend a state of their own even with no hope of help. His life is a reminder that teachers build a state just as soldiers do.
