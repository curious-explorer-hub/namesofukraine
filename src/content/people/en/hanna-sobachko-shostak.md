## Two winters of school

How did a peasant woman who went to school for just two winters end up in the same exhibition as Malevich? Hanna Sobachko was born in 1883 in the village of Skoptsi (then in Poltava province, now in the Kyiv region) into a poor peasant family. From the age of fifteen she painted village houses and paper towels, which her neighbours bought to decorate their homes. She didn't copy flowers from nature: she invented them.

## The workshop at Skoptsi

In 1910 the landowner Anastasiia Semyhradova opened an embroidery and carpet workshop on her estate at Skoptsi and invited the artist Yevheniia Prybylska to run it. Prybylska noticed Hanna's talent. Embroiderers turned Hanna's designs into panels and embroidery, and as early as 1913 they were shown in St Petersburg, Kyiv and Paris, and in 1914 in Berlin. In 1915 the workshop's pieces went to Moscow for an exhibition of contemporary decorative art, which also showed embroidery made from designs by [Kazimir Malevich](/en/people/kazymyr-malevych/) and [Alexandra Exter](/en/people/oleksandra-ekster/).

## The flowers the avant-garde learned from

Her compositions are full of movement: asymmetrical, in bright, intense colours, with flowers that turn into birds. In 1916, in the middle of the First World War, she made a composition called Alarm, which scholars read as an echo of the war. Art historians believe professional artists picked up her methods too. Exter, it is said, borrowed the dynamism and deep indigo backgrounds of her paintings for her costumes for the play Thamyris the Kitharede in Moscow (1916). In 1918 Exter spoke at the opening of a Kyiv exhibition of decorative drawings by Prybylska and Sobachko.

## Far from home

In 1932 Hanna and her family ended up in Cherkizovo, a settlement near Moscow. Sources differ on why: some call it exile, others say the move saved her from the Holodomor famine. There a textile factory made embroidery in the Ukrainian folk style from her drawings. In 1936 she was named a Master of Folk Art. Yet in Soviet times she was labelled only a "master of folk art", which played down her part in the avant-garde. In the early 1960s the journalist Hryhorii Miestiechkin tracked down the artist, by then nearly eighty. She painted a cycle called Flowers of Ukraine, and in 1964 her first and only solo exhibition in her lifetime opened in Kyiv. Hanna Sobachko-Shostak died in Cherkizovo on 3 December 1965.

## Why it matters today

Hanna Sobachko-Shostak showed that bold new art could be born not only in big-city studios but in a village, out of folk painting on a paper towel. Her flowers stand at the roots of the Ukrainian avant-garde: a peasant woman and the innovators learned from each other. Today her work is read again not as "folk craft" but as part of Ukrainian modernism. Anyone who thinks nothing can come of a life without schooling or a capital city can remember the girl from Skoptsi.
