## Why did a teacher walk out of college and take to the road?

Hryhorii Skovoroda could have had a quiet career. Born into a Cossack family in the village of Chornukhy in the Poltava region, he studied at the Kyiv-Mohyla Academy, sang in the court choir in Saint Petersburg, and in 1745 left with a Russian mission to the Tokaj vineyards in Hungary. From there he travelled to Vienna, Buda and Pressburg (now Bratislava) to meet learned people: he spoke fluent Latin and German and knew Greek. Later he taught poetics in Pereiaslav and at the Kharkiv Collegium.

Skovoroda was also a musician: he played the violin, flute, bandura and gusli, and wrote music for church concertos.

But his independent views kept setting him against the church authorities. When he was urged to become a monk and promised a high church rank, he replied: “Do you want me, too, to add to the number of Pharisees?” and gave up teaching. He spent the last quarter-century of his life as a wandering philosopher: he walked across Sloboda Ukraine (Slobozhanshchyna), stayed with friends, wrote and taught, with no property and no official post.

In 1770 he was staying in Kyiv when he suddenly insisted on leaving, though his friends begged him to stay. One day, on his way down to Podil, he caught, in Kovalynskyi’s words, such a strong “smell of dead corpses” that he turned back home, and the next day he set off. Two weeks later, in Okhtyrka, he heard that plague had broken out in Kyiv and the city had been closed.

In 1773 his friend and pupil Mykhailo Kovalynskyi met a scholar in Lausanne, Daniel Meinhard, who was strikingly like Skovoroda in looks, character and way of thinking. When Skovoroda heard about him, he “came to love him without meeting him” and from then on signed his letters and works “Hryhorii Varsava Skovoroda, Daniel Meinhard”. Varsava means “son of Sava”.

## Happiness in “kindred work”

So what did he preach on the road? That happiness lies in knowing yourself and in “kindred work”: the work you have a natural calling for. Don’t chase wealth and fame, he said; live simply, honestly and in harmony with nature. He set out his ideas in dialogues, parables, poems and fables: his Kharkiv Fables has 30 of them. He never published a single book in his lifetime: his works passed around in handwritten copies made by friends and readers.

## Why it matters today

Skovoroda chose freedom over a career: when the bishop pressed him to become a monk, he gave up teaching and spent a quarter-century with no property or post, living by his own views. His idea of “kindred work”, work you have a natural calling for, speaks to anyone choosing a profession. He taught that happiness lies not in wealth and fame but in knowing yourself and living simply and honestly. The words he asked for on his grave, “The world tried to catch me, but did not catch me,” still encourage anyone who won’t live by others’ measure. His portrait is on the 500-hryvnia banknote.
