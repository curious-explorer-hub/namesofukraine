## Who he was

Oles Honchar was born in the village of Sukha in the Poltava region (other sources give Lomivka, now part of Dnipro, and say he was raised in Sukha by his grandparents). He studied at the Kharkiv technical school of journalism and, from 1938, at Kharkiv University. In 1941 he volunteered for the front with a students’ battalion, was wounded twice, and was held in a camp on Kholodna Hora in Kharkiv. After the war he graduated from Dnipropetrovsk University (1946).

## His story

Honchar became widely known for The Standard-Bearers (1946–1948), a war trilogy awarded Stalin Prizes. Later came the novels Man and Arms (1960; Shevchenko Prize, 1962), The Sheep Bell (1963; Lenin Prize, 1964), The Cyclone, The Shore of Love and Your Dawn. In 1959–1971 he headed the Writers’ Union of Ukraine; he was a deputy of the Supreme Soviet of the USSR, from 1973 chaired the Ukrainian Republican Committee for the Defense of Peace, and held the title Hero of Socialist Labor (1978). Encyclopedic assessments of his work differ: some of his books are placed within the canon of socialist realism, while others note a gradual move away from it from the 1960s on.

Official critics condemned the novel The Cathedral (1968), in which an old Cossack cathedral becomes a symbol of the people’s historical memory, and it was withdrawn from literary life for 20 years. As head of the Writers’ Union, Honchar spoke out for the wider use of the Ukrainian language and against Russification. In the late 1980s and early 1990s he supported the founding of a society of Ukrainian-language supporters and the People’s Movement of Ukraine (Rukh), and headed the charitable fund for rebuilding St. Michael’s Golden-Domed Monastery. He died in Kyiv in 1995 and was buried at Baikove Cemetery.

## Why it matters today

Honchar is one of the best-known Ukrainian prose writers of the second half of the 20th century, and his works have been translated into many languages. During the years when Ukrainian statehood was being restored, his authority lent support to Ukrainian democratic forces, and his posthumously published Diaries revealed his national views and his cautious resistance to dogmatic socialist realism.
