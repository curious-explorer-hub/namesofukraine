## Where the letter ґ came from

Did you know the Ukrainian letter ґ has an author? A hooked sign had turned up in manuscripts before, but Meletii Smotrytskyi was the one who put it into the alphabet as a separate letter for the hard [g] sound, distinct from the Ukrainian [h]. He did it in 1619, in a book that went on to teach the language for nearly two hundred years.

## A scholar's son from Ostroh

Meletii, born Maksym, was born around 1577: in Smotrych in Podillia by some accounts, in Ostroh by others. His father Herasym was the first rector of the Ostroh Academy, founded by Prince [Kostiantyn-Vasyl Ostrozkyi](/en/people/kostiantyn-vasyl-ostrozkyi/). The boy studied in Ostroh and then, with the prince's support, at the Jesuit academy in Vilnius. Later, as tutor to a young prince, he attended lectures at the Protestant universities of Leipzig and Wittenberg.

## A lament for the Eastern Church

In 1610 a book in Polish came out in Vilnius: Threnos, or the Lament of the Eastern Church. In it the Orthodox Church, like a mother, mourns the children who have left her: the noble families going over to the Union and to Catholicism. The author signed it with a made-up name, Theophil Orthologus. King Sigismund III ordered the print run destroyed and the author and printers arrested, and Threnos became a rallying cry for the Orthodox. Soon after, Smotrytskyi became a monk in Vilnius, taking the name Meletii.

## A grammar for two centuries

In 1619 his Slavonic Grammar came out in the small town of Vievis near Vilnius. It was a full course in Church Slavonic, the written language of church and learning among the Orthodox Slavs: spelling, inflection, syntax and even verse. It was used in Ukraine and Belarus, and by Serbs, Croats and Romanians. It was reprinted in Moscow too, and Mikhail Lomonosov called it the gateway to his learning.

## An archbishop without a see

In 1620 Patriarch Theophanes of Jerusalem restored the Orthodox hierarchy, and Smotrytskyi was consecrated archbishop of Polatsk. The authorities of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth held this illegal, since Uniate bishops already held those sees, and in 1621 the king ordered his arrest. In 1623 the Uniate archbishop Josaphat Kuntsevych was killed in Vitebsk, and Smotrytskyi was accused of stirring people up against him. Soon he set off for Constantinople and Palestine, and in 1625 he came home disillusioned.

## From Threnos to the Union

In 1627 Smotrytskyi secretly joined the Uniate Church and became archimandrite of the Derman Monastery in Volhynia. He wanted to bring Orthodox and Uniates together in one church with its own patriarch, and negotiated this with Metropolitan Yov Boretskyi and [Petro Mohyla](/en/people/petro-mohyla/). The talks failed. In 1628 a church council in Kyiv condemned his Apologia, a book about his journey to the East, and Smotrytskyi had to renounce his views in public, but soon he openly took the side of the Union. Meletii Smotrytskyi died in Derman on 27 December 1633 and was buried in the monastery there.

## Debates and assessments

Smotrytskyi's move to the Union has been argued over for almost four centuries. His Orthodox contemporaries saw him as an apostate, and the 1628 council anathematised his Apologia. The Uniate bishop Jakub Susza, on the contrary, compared him to the Apostle Paul, the persecutor who became a preacher. Some scholars point to worldly circumstances: he was given the Derman Monastery on condition that he accept the Union. Others believe that in Threnos and in his later works alike Smotrytskyi was looking for the same thing, a way to keep his people and their church united, and that the Union seemed to him to be that way. He himself insisted that he had acted out of love of truth, not for wealth or fame.

## Why it matters today

Smotrytskyi showed that a language becomes a language of learning and schooling once it has clear rules, and he gave those rules to generations of students in several countries. The letter ґ that he brought into the alphabet is still part of Ukrainian spelling. His Threnos showed that a church and a people can be defended with words. And his painful path from Orthodox polemicist to champion of the Union is a reminder of how hard Ukrainians of the 17th century searched for a way to keep their community whole between Rome, Constantinople and foreign rule.
