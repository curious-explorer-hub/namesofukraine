## A surgeon from a drowned village

Mykola Amosov was born in 1913 in the village of Olkhove in the Vologda region of russia. The village is no longer on the map: it was flooded by the Rybinsk Reservoir. Amosov first studied power engineering, but news of his mother’s death gave him the idea of building an artificial heart, and this became one of the reasons he turned to medicine. He studied at two institutes at once, did the first two years of medical school in one year, and graduated from both with honours in 1939. In the Second World War Amosov was the leading surgeon of a field hospital and operated on about 40,000 wounded. In 1952 he moved to Kyiv, where he spent the rest of his life.

## How to fix a heart

In Kyiv Amosov began with lung surgery and then moved on to the heart. In 1958 he was the first in Ukraine to operate with artificial circulation, where a machine takes over the heart’s work for a while. The USSR did not make the fabric he needed, so Amosov sewed his first artificial heart valve leaflet from a nylon shirt he had bought abroad with his own money. In 1963 he was the first in the USSR to replace a mitral heart valve with a prosthesis, and in 1965 he created the world’s first heart valve prostheses designed to prevent blood clots. His clinic grew into an institute of cardiovascular surgery that now bears his name. In time it was performing about 3,000 heart operations a year. Over 48 years of practice Amosov himself performed more than 10,000 operations.

## A doctor who wrote for everyone

Amosov wrote books for general readers, among them Thoughts and the Heart and The Book of Happiness and Misfortune. He developed a system of “limits and loads”, exercise plus moderation in eating, and tested it on himself for years.

## Why it matters today

Mykola Amosov showed that science, hard work and discipline save lives. He took on heart operations no one in Ukraine had done before, and his clinic grew into an institute that has saved thousands of lives. Through his books for general readers he taught everyone to look after their own health. In the 2008 national vote “Great Ukrainians” Amosov came second, with 322,000 votes. Minor planet No. 2948 is named after him, and for his centenary the Verkhovna Rada declared 2013 the Year of Mykola Amosov in medicine; UNESCO also included the anniversary in its calendar of commemorations. His example shows that the courage to do what no one has done before turns into lives saved.
