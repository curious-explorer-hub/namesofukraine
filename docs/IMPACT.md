# Impact endings: review

Audit of the last section of every profile, «Чому це важливо сьогодні» / "Why it matters today" or its hook heading, against [STYLE.md → The ending](STYLE.md#the-ending-why-this-person-matters). Many endings list death, burial, awards, streets and monuments: how a person is remembered, not what they changed. Each ending should say why the person is on the site: what they gave Ukraine, people or the world, and why we can be proud of them.

**Audit, 2026-10-13:** 276 profiles (196 approved, 80 drafts). 148 endings are fine; **128 need work**: 69 are remembrance only, 59 are weak (the impact is thin, buried among memorial facts, or followed by a separate memorial section).

## How review works

- One batch = 5 profiles. The table shows the Ukrainian text; the English version is under each table and says the same things in its own voice.
- New endings use only facts already in the profile and its sources. Links and `related:` stay as they are.
- Mark a batch **approved**, or note changes per profile. Only then are the profile files edited (uk + en), one commit per batch.
- Order: profiles in the social media plan (`social/YYYY-MM.txt`) come first, by post date, so their text is fixed before it goes out; then the other drafted ones; then approved profiles before drafts, remembrance-only before weak.
- The last section is headed «Чому це важливо сьогодні» / "Why it matters today": it is the text posted to social media.

<details><summary>English, batch 1</summary>

- **Alla Horska.** Alla Horska showed that being Ukrainian can be a choice: as an adult she learned the language no one had taught her as a child and made it her own. The Suchasnyk club she helped found became a home for the Sixtiers. With friends she tracked down the places where the NKVD had secretly buried the people it shot, so that the truth about them would not vanish. She believed colour was “the soul, the history of a people”, and she did not stay silent when her friends were put on trial. Even her funeral became a gathering of Sixtiers: [Yevhen Sverstiuk](/en/people/yevhen-sverstiuk/), [Vasyl Stus](/en/people/vasyl-stus/) and others spoke, and they were later arrested.
- **Ivan Kotliarevskyi.** Kotliarevskyi proved that the language people spoke at home and at the market was fit for great literature. Modern Ukrainian literature began with his Eneida, and modern Ukrainian drama with Natalka Poltavka and Moskal-Charivnyk. Great actors, from Mikhail Shchepkin to Mariia Zankovetska, played in them, and Mykola Lysenko set Natalka Poltavka to music. Anyone who writes in Ukrainian today, for a reader or for the stage, follows a road he opened more than two hundred years ago.
- **Ivan Nechui-Levytskyi.** Drop "How he is remembered"; add to the end of "Why it matters today": Kaidash’s Family and Mykola Dzheria are still read today: the Ukrainian village of the 19th century, seen as in a mirror, in a language the empire would not let him print at home.
- **Vasyl Stus.** Keep “How he came back”; add a new last section, “Why it matters today”: Stus gave up neither his words nor his beliefs, though they cost him two sentences and more than 600 poems destroyed. He kept writing: Palimpsests took shape in captivity, and there too he translated Rilke and Goethe. He joined the Ukrainian Helsinki Group knowing that defending human rights meant years in the camps. His life shows that a person can be imprisoned but not made to fall silent. Anyone today who stands up for a truth others want drowned out can lean on his example.
- **Oleksandr Dovzhenko.** Dovzhenko took Ukrainian cinema to world level: Ukrainian national cinema is counted from his Zvenyhora, and critics named Earth among the 12 best films of all time. The question from his diary, “Is love for one’s own people really nationalism?”, still speaks to anyone who has had to stand up for the right to be themselves. That is why the Kyiv film studio, the Oleksandr Dovzhenko National Centre and Ukraine’s State Prize for cinema bear his name.

<details><summary>English, batch 2</summary>

- **Petro Bolbochan.** Petro Bolbochan was among those who built a Ukrainian army in 1917. His Crimean campaign is one of the UNR Army’s most successful operations: a week after his men raced across the Chonhar bridge, Ukrainian flags went up over ships of the Black Sea Fleet in Sevastopol. Kyiv later ordered the troops out at the German command’s demand, but the campaign showed what the young republic’s army could do. He stated his aim plainly in an order to his troops: “We are fighting for an independent democratic Ukrainian Republic, not for a united Russia.” Since 2020 the 3rd Operational Brigade of the National Guard of Ukraine has borne his name.
- **Solomiia Krushelnytska.** Solomiia Krushelnytska showed that a girl from a village in the Ternopil region could become a star of the world’s leading opera stages, recognized alongside Enrico Caruso and Feodor Chaliapin. After Madama Butterfly flopped, she sang the title role so well that Puccini considered her “the most beautiful and most charming Butterfly,” and in Italy she was the first to sing Strauss’s Salome and Elektra. On those same stages she also sang Ukrainian folk songs and the music of [Mykola Lysenko](/en/people/mykola-lysenko/), so the world heard Ukraine in her voice. Back in Lviv, she taught young singers at the conservatory. Fittingly, the Lviv Opera now bears her name.
- **Bohdan Hawrylyshyn.** Bohdan Hawrylyshyn went from a teenage forced laborer and a lumberjack in Canada to director of the International Management Institute in Geneva. His report to the Club of Rome, on how to make societies more effective, was translated into 8 languages. When Ukraine became independent, he brought that experience home: he chaired a council advising the parliament’s Presidium, co-chaired the International Renaissance Foundation and co-founded the International Management Institute in Kyiv. His life bears out his own advice: “dream, but dream big, about great things.” You can start with almost nothing, and still bring what you learn in the world back to your own country.
- **Oleksii Mes.** Oleksii Mes campaigned for the very jets he would later fly to defend Ukraine. With his friend [Andrii Pilshchykov](/en/people/andrii-pilshchykov/) he explained to American politicians why Ukraine urgently needed Patriots, ATACMS and F-16s, and the two classmates became its faces. In 2023 he trained on the F-16 himself. On 26 August 2024, during one of Russia’s most powerful missile strikes, he destroyed three cruise missiles and an attack drone before they could reach their targets. He showed that a pilot can defend his country both in the cockpit and in the rooms where help is decided. And in Kharkiv, where they studied, part of a lane named Hlisada Dzhusa (“Juice’s Glide Path”) was renamed Moonfish Alley in 2025: the two friends’ names sit side by side on the city map.
- **Mariia Prymachenko.** Mariia Prymachenko taught herself to paint, yet she created a world of her own: flowers in unusual colors, birds as signs of goodness and peace, and “beasts” that don’t exist. She made more than 800 works. She showed that art the world honors can grow in a village in Polissia. In 2022 a museum guard in Ivankiv and his wife carried her paintings out of the fire under shelling; since then her beasts and doves became a symbol of Ukraine’s resilience. Her caption on a poppy painting reads as a wish to us all: “I give red poppies to people, so they would love the holy land and work on it.”

- **Ivan Marchuk.** Ivan Marchuk created his own painting language: plontanism, pictures of fine interwoven lines that seem to glow, is almost impossible to copy. And in 1907, with Mykola Sadovskyi, she founded the first permanent Ukrainian theater in Kyiv, giving the Ukrainian stage a home of its own. Today the Ukrainian drama theater in Lviv bears her name.

He showed that one person’s choice at the right moment can buy time for many others. His deed is known beyond Ukraine too: Prague has a Vitalii Skakun Bridge.

<details><summary>English, batch 8</summary>

- **Ihor Kostenko.** Ihor Kostenko lived by his own words: “Everyone should do what they can, where they are.” What he could do was write, and in two and a half years he added more than 280 articles to the Ukrainian Wikipedia, on geography, the history of Ukraine’s armed forces, economics and football. He didn’t only write; he brought in new contributors, and the community did hold his wiki flash mob in the end, in his memory. And he went to the Maidan himself. Every article someone adds to the Ukrainian Wikipedia today carries his work on; in 2014 Jimmy Wales named him Wikipedian of the Year.
- **Bohdan Solchanyk.** Bohdan Solchanyk brought together scholarship, teaching and civic action. As a historian he studied how ordinary people vote in a small town; as a lecturer he taught students modern Ukrainian history. And he brought students out onto the streets himself: with Pora! in 2004, and in 2013 at the head of a column of several thousand marching for the EU agreement. Yet he cared that protest should not cost lives: a friend recalled him talking people out of storming a building, because people could be killed. His friends published his poems as Kitchens of My Generation, and his memory went back to scholarship: the Ukrainian Catholic University created its first doctoral scholarship in his name.
- **Ivan Mykolaichuk.** Playing Ivan in Shadows of Forgotten Ancestors and [Taras Shevchenko](/en/people/taras-shevchenko/) in The Dream made Mykolaichuk one of the faces of Ukrainian poetic cinema. But he didn’t stay just an actor: he wrote screenplays, and in Babylon XX and Such a Late, Such a Warm Autumn he was director, co-writer, music arranger and one of the cast all at once. A boy from a Bukovyna village showed that Ukrainian cinema could be made from the first word of the script to the last note. Anyone who discovers these films today meets him in them.
- **Anatolii Solovianenko.** Solovianenko proved that a voice from a Donbas coal miner’s family could fill La Scala and the Metropolitan Opera. He only turned professional at 30, after years of teaching at an institute, which shows it is never too late to find your calling. At the opening of Expo 67 in Montreal he sang Verdi and Puccini, and alongside them “Povii, Vitre, na Vkrainu” and “Dyvlius Ya na Nebo”. All his life he sang Ukrainian songs and romances, recorded 18 LPs and, in his own words, put “his whole soul” into them. In 2008 he was posthumously made a Hero of Ukraine.
- **Volodymyr Sosiura.** A boy from the Third Company who worked as a labourer from the age of 11 became a lyric poet whose very first collections made him famous. In the 1930s, the years of the purges, he was almost the only Ukrainian poet who kept writing about love. His Mazepa was hushed up, his collection Heart was confiscated, and “Love Ukraine!” got him accused of “bourgeois nationalism” and threatened with arrest. [Ivan Bahrianyi](/en/people/ivan-bahrianyi/) wrote that Sosiura was in constant conflict with the authorities because of his love for his people. Today the poem is read and learned again, and its words “Love Ukraine as you love the sun” belong to everyone who says them.
