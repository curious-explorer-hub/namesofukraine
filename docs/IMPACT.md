# Impact endings: review

Audit of the last section of every profile, «Чому це важливо сьогодні» / "Why it matters today" or its hook heading, against [STYLE.md → The ending](STYLE.md#the-ending-why-this-person-matters). Many endings list death, burial, awards, streets and monuments: how a person is remembered, not what they changed. Each ending should say why the person is on the site: what they gave Ukraine, people or the world, and why we can be proud of them.

**Audit, 2026-10-13:** 276 profiles (196 approved, 80 drafts). 148 endings are fine; **128 need work**: 69 are remembrance only, 59 are weak (the impact is thin, buried among memorial facts, or followed by a separate memorial section).

## How review works

- One batch = 5 profiles. The table shows the Ukrainian text; the English version is under each table and says the same things in its own voice.
- New endings use only facts already in the profile and its sources. Links and `related:` stay as they are.
- Mark a batch **approved**, or note changes per profile. Only then are the profile files edited (uk + en), one commit per batch.
- Order: batches 1–4 sample each kind (writers and artists, fallen defenders, civic figures); after that approved profiles come first, remembrance-only before weak, then drafts.

<details><summary>English, batch 1</summary>

- **Alla Horska.** Alla Horska showed that being Ukrainian can be a choice: as an adult she learned the language no one had taught her as a child and made it her own. The Suchasnyk club she helped found became a home for the Sixtiers. With friends she tracked down the places where the NKVD had secretly buried the people it shot, so that the truth about them would not vanish. She believed colour was “the soul, the history of a people”, and she did not stay silent when her friends were put on trial. Even her funeral became a gathering of Sixtiers: [Yevhen Sverstiuk](/en/people/yevhen-sverstiuk/), [Vasyl Stus](/en/people/vasyl-stus/) and others spoke, and they were later arrested.
- **Ivan Kotliarevskyi.** Kotliarevskyi proved that the language people spoke at home and at the market was fit for great literature. Modern Ukrainian literature began with his Eneida, and modern Ukrainian drama with Natalka Poltavka and Moskal-Charivnyk. Great actors, from Mikhail Shchepkin to Mariia Zankovetska, played in them, and Mykola Lysenko set Natalka Poltavka to music. Anyone who writes in Ukrainian today, for a reader or for the stage, follows a road he opened more than two hundred years ago.
- **Ivan Nechui-Levytskyi.** Drop "How he is remembered"; add to the end of "Why it matters today": Kaidash’s Family and Mykola Dzheria are still read today: the Ukrainian village of the 19th century, seen as in a mirror, in a language the empire would not let him print at home.
- **Vasyl Stus.** Keep “How he came back”; add a new last section, “Why it matters today”: Stus gave up neither his words nor his beliefs, though they cost him two sentences and more than 600 poems destroyed. He kept writing: Palimpsests took shape in captivity, and there too he translated Rilke and Goethe. He joined the Ukrainian Helsinki Group knowing that defending human rights meant years in the camps. His life shows that a person can be imprisoned but not made to fall silent. Anyone today who stands up for a truth others want drowned out can lean on his example.
- **Oleksandr Dovzhenko.** Dovzhenko took Ukrainian cinema to world level: Ukrainian national cinema is counted from his Zvenyhora, and critics named Earth among the 12 best films of all time. The question from his diary, “Is love for one’s own people really nationalism?”, still speaks to anyone who has had to stand up for the right to be themselves. That is why the Kyiv film studio, the Oleksandr Dovzhenko National Centre and Ukraine’s State Prize for cinema bear his name.

</details>

<details><summary>English, batch 2</summary>

- **Dmytro Kotsiubailo.** *What’s inside you.* Dmytro Kotsiubailo went from an 18-year-old volunteer to a battalion commander. After a bad wound he didn’t leave the front: he came back and led his fighters for years, and when the full-scale war began his “Da Vinci Wolves” company grew into a separate battalion. He became the first volunteer made a Hero of Ukraine in his lifetime, and showed how much one person who chose to fight could give the army. He put the heart of it himself: “A fighter isn’t just muscles and a rifle. What matters is what’s inside you, what you’re fighting for.” Thousands came to Maidan Nezalezhnosti to say goodbye to him.
- **Ivan Zubkov.** *Commander and armorer.* Ivan Zubkov could have stayed a civilian: he had been out of the army for sixteen years and had his own business. After the Maidan he volunteered anyway. In his battalion he made sure every comrade’s weapon worked, and at Donetsk airport he led his platoon to relieve the defenders who were cut off there. Wounded, he stayed in the fight and covered his unit’s withdrawal. He showed that a commander answers for everyone beside him, and that experience gained long ago can be needed by the country again. The 90th Battalion he served in now bears his name.
- **Inna Derusova.** *She stayed.* Inna Derusova saved people with more than her own hands. In the Donbas she did the work of three, and she could turn medics fresh from civilian life into real combat medics in two weeks, so her skill lived on in everyone she trained. On the first day of the full-scale war, on her way back to her unit, she found many wounded in Okhtyrka and stayed with them. In two days she saved more than ten soldiers. She was made a Hero of Ukraine posthumously, and the President called her the first woman to receive the title that way.
- **Iryna Tsybukh.** *Where people were needed.* Iryna Tsybukh went where she was needed most. She taught media literacy to children in remote villages of Donbas and told their story in her film “Distance”. At the front her crew carried the wounded straight from the front line; they refitted their vehicle so a wounded soldier was warm straight away, and filmed it so other crews could learn. She dreamed of a house and tomatoes, but believed that ending the war mattered most. And about remembrance she left words meant for us: “Honoring is not for the fallen, but for those who will go on keeping their memory.”
- **Vitalii Skakun.** *The time he won.* Vitalii Skakun had a welder’s trade, a job in Poland and software-engineering studies, and he chose to serve as a combat engineer. On the first day of the full-scale invasion he volunteered to blow up the bridge near Henichesk. The blast slowed a Russian tank column considerably, and Ukrainian troops had time to regroup and set up a defence. He showed that one person’s choice at the right moment can buy time for many others. His deed is known beyond Ukraine too: Prague has a Vitalii Skakun Bridge.

Pidmohylnyi wrote a novel about what happens to someone who leaves the village for the big city, a question anyone who has ever moved will recognise. He translated Balzac, Maupassant, Anatole France, Stendhal and others into Ukrainian and edited multi-volume editions of Balzac and France, and those translations had a marked influence on Ukrainian literature in the 1920s. With [Yevhen Pluzhnyk](/en/people/yevhen-pluzhnyk/) he compiled a dictionary of business language, and his stories bear witness to the famine of 1921–1923. He was shot, but his books came back: The City was published in Ukraine again in 1989, and his work is read in English, German and Polish.

</details>
